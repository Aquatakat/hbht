# Rendering

How the runtime draws scenes as the N64 does, and what's left. The runtime's other stand-ins are in [runtime](runtime.md).

## On-screen values

The N64 has no linear light. Its texels, vertex colours, tints and blends are the values it puts on screen, and the game turns the VI's gamma off (`osViSetSpecialFeatures` in `gfx.cpp`), so the emulator shows the framebuffer as it is. Godot works in linear light, where the same numbers come out differently:

- **Multiplying.** A texel times a vertex shade of 0.5 is half as bright on the N64. Treated as linear, the shade shows at about 73%.
- **Blending.** Godot blends in linear light. A surface at 10% over dark stone adds far more than 10% of its brightness there (Gwernia Castle's window light, 0x1577 at alpha 0.1, came out as harsh white sheets), and black over the scene darkens about half as much (the player's shadow, 31%, looked like 15%).

**Every float colour, alpha and volume becomes a byte the same way,** as 1.0 does it and `ColorBytes` does: multiplied, truncated toward zero (`trunc.w.s`, `trunc.w.d`; the code segment has no rounding instruction), and the low byte kept, so a negative wraps rather than clamps. What differs by site is the multiply's precision, which follows the original's literal: `255.0f` in single (`ColorBytes.Single`) for the sky, particles, HUD, shadows, lights and fades, and a `255.0` double (`ColorBytes.Double`) for the rain loop, the scene objects' view-cone alpha and the audio keys. Sums before the multiply that use a double literal (0.65, 0.15, 0.2, 0.35, 1.5) are worked in double too.

The scene export decodes vertex colours and untextured tints as sRGB, so they're correct glTF (`COLOR_0` is linear) and a viewer that multiplies in linear light comes out almost as the N64 does. The N64 material goes further and works in on-screen values itself.

**The runtime's 3D image holds on-screen values,** so Godot's blender mixes them as the N64's does. The N64 material writes its on-screen colour without turning it linear (`N64Material.OnScreenFramebuffer`), and the environment's colour correction is a table that decodes sRGB (`OnScreenOutput`). Godot applies colour correction after it encodes the image for the screen, so the two cancel and the screen gets the values as drawn. The sky's backdrop is a canvas that Godot decodes as it takes it in behind the scene, so a full-screen pass at the top of its layer encodes it first. The canvas has 8 bits a channel, so near white, neighbouring shades of the sky can merge. Everything translucent blends this way: water, the faded levels of detail, fire and the shadow (which darkens the ground to 0.68 of itself at its middle, the N64's 1 − 80/255). The editor still works in linear light, since Godot's lights light it.

## The N64 material

`N64Material` (in the runtime, linked into the Inspector) replaces every material Godot's glTF import makes, in the runtime and in the Inspector's previews. It builds each from the glTF's own material, because Godot's import converts colours and samplers in ways the N64 doesn't.

- **The combiner.** `setStaticMode` sets textured draws to `TEXEL0 × SHADE`, then `× PRIMITIVE` in the second cycle, and untextured ones to `SHADE × PRIMITIVE` (combine modes `0x127E03, 0xFF0FF3FF` and `0xFFFE03, 0xFF0E79FF`). Alpha is texel alpha times prim alpha, or shade alpha times prim alpha: a textured draw ignores its vertices' alpha. The shader reads raw texels (no `source_color`), turns the vertex colour back into on-screen values and multiplies there. In the runtime it hands Godot the on-screen result (see [on-screen values](#on-screen-values)), and in the editor the linear one.
- **The blender** (`Scene.SetRenderMode`, `0x80097618`, which sets every scene draw's render mode and prim colour). Prim alpha is the scene's alpha × (1 − the mesh tint's alpha) × (1 − the model's alpha), truncated to a byte: the castle's light at 0.1 is 25, Gwernia's sea at 0.5 is 127. The draw is skipped when either alpha of the model or mesh is 1. Under 255 it's blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8`): an ordinary blend, `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, depth tested and not written, whatever the texture. At 255, a texture with transparent texels (Borg1 flag 0x20) is blended if it's IA16, IA8 or RGBA32 and cut out otherwise (`G_RM_AA_ZB_TEX_EDGE2`, `0x113078`, coverage times texel alpha), and everything else is opaque (`G_RM_AA_ZB_OPA_SURF2`, `0x112078`). There's no alpha compare anywhere (`G_AC_NONE`, and no `G_SETBLENDCOLOR`), so the cut-outs' edge is the coverage's, which the port's 0.5 threshold stands in for. Every scene starts from the static state list at `0x800E4758` (`Scene.InitDrawState`, `0x80097300`), and each display list adds its own (`SetStaticMode`, `0x800973b8`).
- **The original's dither.** Translucent draws on the emulator look grainy: the RDP's colour dither (`G_CD_MAGICSQ`, a fixed 4 × 4 pattern) as it writes each pixel to the 16-bit framebuffer (5 bits a channel; `StartGfxList` sets it), with the VI's dither filter on (`osViSetSpecialFeatures(0x62)`: gamma off, divot off, dither filter on). The port draws at full precision and doesn't dither, so its blends are smooth.
- **Order.** The original draws a scene's display lists in its instructions' order, translucent ones where they fall, and sorts nothing. Godot draws translucent surfaces after opaque ones, sorted by distance, which can differ where two translucent surfaces overlap. Across scenes, the original draws a frame in this order (`RenderZones`, `0x8000d574`, and `HandleZoneEngineFrame`): the sky; each neighbour chunk in view, its scene and then its objects; the centre chunk's scene; the particles of emitters flagged for the first pass, which none of the scenes' are; the bodies' shadows; bodies in water; the centre chunk's objects; translucent bodies in water; the other bodies; the chunks' second scenes, such as water (`RenderTransZones`, `0x8000dd44`); then the scene objects that are fading or flagged `Deferred` (`Zones.DrawDeferredScenes`, `0x8000dc98`); then every other emitter's particles (`ProcessAndRenderParticleHead`, `0x800a842c`), and the weather.
- **Texel centres.** The RDP's bilinear filter centres texel *k* on coordinate *k*, where a GPU's centres it on *k* + 0.5, so the material moves every coordinate half a texel before wrapping or clamping. Without it, a shape whose coordinates stop just inside a texture's edge blends in the far side's texels, wrapped round: Shadow Self Merge's pool of blood (IA16 0x2417, transparent in its first column and opaque in its last) runs from S = 0.16 texels and gets a dark line along its edge. Cut-outs such as trees' and Oriana's path lights' edges take the same shift. The particles' and the UI's own shaders don't: a particle's texture is clear all round its quad's edge.
- **Mirrored models** <a id="mirrored-models"></a>show their insides. The RSP culls a triangle by the way it winds on screen, whatever its matrix, so a model whose world matrix mirrors it (an odd number of negative scales up its transforms) has its front culled and its back drawn. Godot turns the culling round for a mirrored instance. The importer marks the scenes whose transforms can mirror a model, at rest or as an animation scales them (the glTF's `mirrors`, 32 scenes: cinematic shots, such as Shadow Self Merge's Shadow, and actors), and `MirroredMeshes` draws a mesh whose world matrix mirrors it with copies of its one-sided materials that cull the other side (`N64Material.Mirrored`), checked each time the scene is posed.
- **Shade** is the vertex colour on unlit display lists. On lit ones it's the RSP's lighting (see [bodies' light](#bodies-light) below), which the runtime works out per vertex (`N64Material.RspLighting`). The Inspector still lets Godot's lights stand in (Lambert diffuse, no specular).
- **Prim** is the scene's tint, `SceneData.colorFloats`, times the mesh tint for untextured draws (`Scene.SetRenderMode`). Every chunk scene and scene object gets `gMainColor` (`SetSceneColors`), which the sky sets (below). A scene object with the `Tint` flag and a non-zero tint gets the average of the two instead (1,398 objects). In `Zones.SetSceneColors` (`0x8000c298`), `RenderVoxelScenes` passes the object's tint word (+0x64) only when the flag is set, and when the whole word, alpha and all, isn't 0, each channel is `(gMainColor + tint) >> 1`, an even average truncated, with nothing clamped or weighted. That's then scaled by the screen fade's brightness (`0x8004d220` with `ScreenFade.Alpha`, 1 when nothing fades), which the untinted path doesn't do. I08, where a new game starts, has 15 of Oriana's path lights tinted a pale cyan (153, 236, 255), and M07 has a birch tinted grey (88, 85, 86). Bodies get white and are lit instead (`some_player_render_sub`, `set_sun_light`), so actors' meshes are marked as bodies.
- **Instance uniforms are `Vector4`s, not `Color`s.** Godot turns a `Color` set on an instance uniform linear, so the tint (`N64Material.SetObjectTint`) and prim (`TextureScrolls`' copies) are set as `Vector4`. Likewise `Resource.Duplicate()` resets a `Color` set on a `vec4` shader parameter to the uniform's default. A light's billboard (`0x374A`, a white IA texture with texel alpha up to 210, blended as any transparent texture is, nothing additive) shows prim over the dark forest floor: at 18:40, prim (154, 189, 199) from `gMainColor` (156, 142, 144).
- **Fog** is per vertex, as the RSP computes it. The amount is the depth buffer's `z / w` for the original's planes, on the 0–1000 scale `gSPFogPosition` takes (0 is `z / w` of −1, 1000 is 1), between the fog's start and end. Draws blend toward the fog colour in on-screen values before the pixel is written, as `G_RM_FOG_SHADE_A` does. In the editor, where Godot's lights light the lit ones, those use Godot's `FOG` output, after its lighting, in linear light. With trek's planes of 1 and 180 and no fog in the weather, the fog runs from 996 to 1000, which is from about 105 units to the far plane. The weather's fog moves the start in to 996 − trunc(`fogTime` × 40), as far as 966 (`Zones.SetSceneColors`, `0x8000c298`; [weather](weather.md#the-fog)), except while a cut-scene plays. The runtime's follow camera stops at 180, as the original's does.
- **Wrapping.** Borg2 materials clamp, repeat or mirror S and T separately (faces are often half a texture, mirrored), which a `BaseMaterial3D` can't, so the shader wraps each axis itself.
- **Globals.** The tint, the fog colour and the fog's range are global shader parameters (`n64_tint`, `n64_fog_color`, `n64_fog`), untinted and unfogged until the runtime sets them. The Inspector leaves them so.

## The sky's colours

Every frame, `Sky::RenderSky` sets five colours from the sky gradient of the terrain the player is in, and the scenery is multiplied by the first. `Aidyn.Core.Sky` ports it, and the runtime's `SkyView` applies it each tick.

- **The gradients** are eight Borg8s, 12 × 37 RGBA32 (`sky_borg8s`, read from the code segment). Rows 0–31 are the sky from top to bottom. Rows 32–36 are the tint, sky, fog, cloud and sun colours. There's a column every two hours from midnight, and `Sky::GenerateSky` blends between the two columns either side of the time of day, wrapping from 22:00 to midnight.
- **The tint** (`gMainColor`) is `tint row × 0.65 + 0.15`, plus a fifth of the sky colour, clamped and truncated to bytes (`Sky::SetColors`, `SkyColors.At`, `0x8001cee0`), the grey and the fifth worked in double and cast to float, then scaled by the screen's brightness and the sky's slower fade in turn (`Sky.Brightness`, `0x800D9518`, which `Sky.FadeBrightness`, `0x8001DE88`, moves: a spell's cast dims it to half and back, the port's `SkyBrightness`, which also scales the backdrop) (a tint row of 255 gives 204). The tint row is grey: 88 at midnight, 225 at 10:00 and noon. So at a new game's noon on terrain 2, the scenery is multiplied by (222, 225, 227). Lens flare darkens it by half its value while the camera faces the sun (see [the sky's objects](#the-skys-objects)). The time of day is one multiplier over all the scenery.
- **Which sky.** `BorgMaps::GetMapTerrain` picks a sky number by terrain in a switch, which the importer carries as a fact on terrain records. Terrain types 6, 8, 9, 11 and 13–16 don't draw the gradient: the background is black, there's no weather, but the colours still come from it. Every new chunk the player enters chooses again (`Sky::SetBackgroundType` with 600), which fades to a different sky over 600 ticks. Choosing again restarts the fade, and choosing the sky that's up abandons it. Into or out of a sky that isn't drawn, between two that aren't, and as trek's world is built, the sky changes at once (`LoadGameBorgScenes`, `0x8000b05c`, `SkyState.Enter`). Rain and snow bring the rain sky and hold it against entering chunks ([weather](weather.md#the-rain-sky)).
- **The clock.** A new game starts at noon on day 2 of week 6 (`InitGameGlobals`), and trek time runs 72 times as fast as real time, so a day takes 20 minutes. `--time <hours>` starts at another time, and the overlay shows it.
- **The backdrop.** Behind the scenery, `Sky::GenerateGradient` draws rows 0–31 of the gradient down the screen, a line at a time, on the original's 320 × 240 screen (`GetVRes` is always 240). `SkyBackdrop` ports it and `SkyBackdropView` draws it, on a canvas layer the environment shows as the background, scaled to the window's height. The gradient spans 200 lines, from row 1/200 of the way down at the top of the screen to its foot at line 200 when the camera looks level. Looking down moves it up by sin(y) × 240 lines, where y is the height of the look's unit vector, so the sine of a sine: 61 lines at the follow camera's usual 15°. Looking up doesn't move it down. Below the foot the screen takes the foot's colour, and looking down so far that no line is left (sin(y) × 240 over 200) leaves it black. It isn't projected, so it only roughly meets the scenery's horizon, and the far plane hides the difference.
- **The projected dome.** Under `SkyPlacement.Projected`, the default, the runtime projects the gradient as a dome instead (`SkyBackdrop.DomeFraction`, drawn per pixel by a shader in `SkyBackdropView`): each pixel takes the gradient's line for the elevation of the way through it, so its rows stay fixed against the scenery, as the sun and clouds do. Each elevation keeps the line the original gives it down the middle of the screen at the follow camera's usual look (14.5° down, where the original moves the gradient up 59 lines): the horizon is 0.52 of the way down the gradient, its top is 17.1° up, above which the sky is its top colour, and its foot 18.5° under the horizon, behind the ground. At that pitch the two placements agree within a level or two. They part as the camera pitches, and the follow camera pitches 0°–45°, so the dome is what keeps the sky from sliding against the scenery and the sun. `--sky original` or F6 draws the original's. The weather's fog fades the backdrop to the fog colour, fully from half thick, lens flare lifts it, and lightning fills the screen white in its place for a frame ([weather](weather.md#lightning-and-thunder)). The canvas passes on-screen values through.

## The sky's objects

An outdoor sky draws, in order: the gradient, then the stars, the sun and the moon (`Skyobjects::Render`), then the clouds (`Clouds::Render`), then the scenery, then the lens flare (`Lensflare::Render`), then the HUD. The tables come from the ROM on the sky record, `aidyn:sky/0` (see the [importer](importer.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Sky` has `SkyObjects`, `Clouds` and `LensFlare`; `SkyBackdropView` draws the first two behind the scene and `LensFlareView` the flare over it. All are sprites on the 320 × 240 screen, texel × colour with texel alpha × alpha, blended normally (`borg8DlistInit` with flag 6: combine `0xFC119623 FF2FFFFF`, `TEXEL0 × PRIMITIVE` for both, and `G_RM_XLU_SURF2`), with bilinear filtering. Godot's 2D blends in on-screen values too.

- **Placement isn't projected.** Each object has two angles, a height and an azimuth. From the camera's look, x is 160 plus 300 × the sine of the azimuth against the look's heading, and y is 120 plus 300 × the sine of the elevation against the look's height. Perspective puts the scenery at 289.7 × the tangent instead (120 / tan 22.5°, for the Borg3 cameras' 45°). The two agree in the middle of the screen, but toward its sides the scenery turns up to about 30% faster than the sky, so the clouds, sun, moon and stars drag along with the camera. The azimuth turns half round while the height is between 90° and 270°, so an object carries on over the top. The elevation is sin(height) × 90°, not the height, so the sun is drawn at about 64° at 09:00 while it lights from 45°. The sprite's top-left corner goes at the point, not its middle.
- **The projected placement** is the default (`SkyPlacement.Projected`; `--sky original` or F6 for the original's), the user's choice. It gives each sprite a direction (its azimuth and elevation) and projects it as the scenery is projected (`SkyScreen.Project`: a 45° pinhole, pitch and all), so the sky stays pinned against the scenery at any pitch, like a skybox. Each sprite is a patch of sky: laid flat, facing the camera, a unit away in its direction, kept upright against the horizon, and sized so it's drawn as the original draws it when the camera looks straight at it (`SkyPatch`). Its corners are projected like the scenery and it's drawn as a 4 × 4 grid of triangles, so the texture follows the perspective. The sky reads the camera's interpolated transform, as the scenery is drawn from, not the last tick's.
- **The sun**'s height is the time of day × 360° − 90°, so it's on the horizon at 06:00 and 18:00, and its azimuth is 90°: it rises in +X, as its light does. It's drawn in the sky's sun colour at alpha 250, at scale 1 (64 pixels). Where its middle lands is kept for the lens flare, and kept while it isn't drawn.
- **The moon**'s images are IA8 with every intensity at full, so each is a white disc shaped by its alpha, with no surface. Its height is twice (time of day × 360° + 90°), so it goes round twice a day, and it sways 40° either side of its azimuth of 0. Its image is its phase (`World::set_moonPhase`) on a cycle of four weeks, with the first two counted as waxing, though the images show it waning then: full on days 0–2, a quarter on 3–7, a crescent on 9–13, new on 14–16, a crescent on 17–21 and a quarter on 23–27, with days 8 and 22 turning at noon. A new game starts under a new moon (week 6, day 2: day 16), whose image is faint (alpha at most 85).
- **The stars**, 91 of them, show only while the sun is down, from 18:00 to 06:00, at the sine of how far down it is × their alpha, so they come out as it sets. Each frame each takes a random grey from 160 to 255, so they twinkle: the port draws the grey once per rendered frame from a stream of its own (`SkyView.Twinkle`, the original's generator from the boot seed, not saved), so the game's streams don't depend on the frame rate. `Skyobjects::Init` sets their scale to 0.19 over the table's 0.2. One star has alpha 0 in the ROM and never shows.
- **The clouds**, 55 of them, are points on a plane within 750 of the camera, drawn in the sky's cloud colour. A cloud's nearness is 1 − distance / 750, and it's drawn 240 × nearness above the horizon line (120 + 300 × the look's height), at nearness + 0.2 times its size, with alpha × √nearness; it's centred by nearness, not by its scale, so slightly off. Projected, a cloud is a direction: its heading on the plane, at the elevation the original draws it at when the camera looks straight at it, where the original's sines and perspective agree. 240 × nearness pixels at 300 to the unit is a sine of 0.8 × nearness, from the horizon at the edge of the plane to 53° up over the camera. The plane goes with the camera, so no distance is involved: walking and turning don't move a cloud against the scenery, only the wind does. The game takes the way to the camera with X and Z swapped against the sky objects' azimuths, which only mirrors the layout. The wind blows them 4 × its velocity a frame, which the port spreads over the frame's ticks, and one that goes past 750 comes back in on the far side. The wind is [weather](weather.md#the-wind)'s (`Weather.DriftWind`). They fade in and out as the cloud cover passes each one's threshold, but nothing changes the cover from 1, so all show.
- **The weather's fog** fades the sun by its thickness, and the moon, stars and clouds faster (× 1.5, 2 and 1.5), as the [weather](weather.md) has it.
- **The lens flare** shows while the sun's middle is on screen, 10 pixels inside the edges, and nothing hides it. It fades in by 10 a frame up to 50 and out the same, over 5 frames. Its eight pieces are drawn along the line from the middle of the screen through the sun, at their distance along it, at their scale × the sun's distance from the middle / 80 up to 1, and not at all at 0.2 or less, in their colours at alpha fade × the tint's red. Within 80 of the middle it also sets `lensFlareVal`, the tint's red × (1 − distance / 80) × 0.5 × fade / 50, which next frame lowers the tint by half of it and lifts the gradient by all of it. The original tests the depth buffer under the sun's middle at the end of each frame, for the next. The port sends a ray through that point against the collision, out to the far plane, which catches the ground and buildings but not leaves.

## Bodies' light

The sky lights bodies and animated scene objects; scenery it only tints. `Aidyn.Core.Sky.SkyLight` has the pieces, and the N64 material lights each vertex from global parameters that `SkyView` sets each tick.

- **The RSP's lighting** is the ambient plus each light's colour times the cosine between the vertex's normal and the way to the light, where that's positive, clamped to 1, in on-screen values, per vertex. The game puts the camera in the projection matrix (`gsAnimationDataMtx` loads it with `G_MTX_PROJECTION`), so the modelview is the model alone and the lights are in world space.
- **Three kinds of lit mesh** (`N64Material.Lighting`). Bodies, the player among them, get white prim, an ambient of half `gSkyColor` truncated to bytes, and the sun (`some_player_render_sub`). Scene objects' actors get white prim, `gMainColor` as ambient, and the sun (`set_sun_light`), unless they're fullbright, when they're tinted like scenery. Scenery's lit meshes keep the white ambient `Scene::SetLightData` gives every scene and no other light, so they come out as the tint alone. Bodies and actors also get the [point lights](#point-lights) that reach them.
- **The sun** is `gSunColor` from the direction `Skyobjects::UpdateSun` works out: at an angle of the time of day × 360° − 90° about the Z axis, turned by the sun's 90° in the sky objects' table. It rises in +X at 06:00, is overhead at noon and sets in −X at 18:00, and lights from below at night, when the gradient's sun colour is dark. Only an outdoor sky has it; in caves (`SkyType4`) bodies get the ambient alone.
- **Which copy of the colour.** `Scene::addLight` writes the colour into `colc`, the second of the two copies in `Light_t`, and zeroes `col` (bytes 0–3 zero, 4–6 the colour and 8–10 the direction, × 120). F3DEX2 reads `colc`, so the port takes the colour as the game means it: the sun does light Alaron.
- **The moon** adds a light whenever it's up (`Sky.MoonDirection`'s y, `0x800D953C`, over 0, from 06:00 to noon and 18:00 to midnight) and the sky is outdoor, after the sun: from the moon's way (its height and its table azimuth, without the sway it's drawn with, `SkyObjects.UpdateMoon`, `0x8002A090`), cyan, (0, g, g) with g = trunc(200 × the moon's height × the screen's fade × the body's tint level) (`Bodies.SetLights`). Outside combat the level is 0, so it's black, but it still takes one of the body's four lights. The port does the same (`WorldLights.Moon`, `MoonLight`, `SkyLight.MoonDirection`).
- **The tint** (body `+0x75C` level, `+0x760` rate, `+0x764` colour), which only combat changes: each frame `ProcessPlayers` moves the level by its rate and, while either isn't 0, moves the ambient from half the sky's colour toward the colour by the level, × the screen's fade, clamped (`0x80012B1C`). The target marks set it to a grey pulse and a cast glows it white ([combat](combat.md#what-spells-look-like)); the port's is `Combatant.Tint`, which the runtime hands the N64 material as the body's own ambient.
- **Point lights** add more: light map objects and dynamic lights (below), as directional lights from the body toward each.

## Point lights

Light map objects (777 in the base game) and the game's dynamic lights light bodies and the actors maps place, as directional lights worked out for each draw. `Aidyn.Core.Lights` ports them (`PointLight`, `WorldLights`, `SceneLights`, `DynamicLights`), and the runtime's `Game.LightBodies` hands each lit model its lights every tick, which the N64 material adds to the sun and ambient per vertex (`N64Material.SetLights`, up to four).

- **How many.** A scene has room for 7 lights and an ambient (`Scene.ResetLights`, `0x8009e778`). `SceneSetMaxDynamicDirLights` (`0x8009e7d0`) lowers it to 4 for bodies (`RenderPlayers`, `0x800140f0`) and 2 for actors a map places (`RenderVoxelScenes`, `0x8000cd3c`); nothing else calls it. The sun comes first while the sky is outdoor, then the moon while it's up, so a body outdoors has 3 for point lights, or 2 with the moon up, and an actor 1. Once the room is full, a new light takes the place of the dimmest one after the sun and moon by r² + g² + b² (`Scene.LightBrightness`, `0x8009e800`) if that's dimmer, and is dropped otherwise (`SceneLights.Add`, `0x8009e838`, which ignores the brightness byte it's passed). The colour goes in the second copy, `colc`, and the direction × 120 (see above). The draw (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`) hands the RSP the lights and the ambient, and empties the list.
- **What's lit.** A body (`Bodies.SetLights`, `0x80013798`) gets the sun, the moon, the dynamic lights (`Lights.AddDynamic`, `0x8004d9e0`), and then, only if it's one of the party's (`+0x70a`, which `InitGameTrek` sets on the player and combat on the party's members, not their enemies), the light map objects that the grid cell under its centre lists in its own chunk (`WorldLights.ForBody`, `0x8004d878`). So in trek, only the player is lit by map lights; wanderers get the dynamic lights alone. In a battle the party's members would be, but the Battle arenas have no light map objects. An actor a map places that isn't fullbright gets the sun, the dynamic lights and every light map object of its chunk (`MapObjects.SetActorLights`, `0x80013ac4`, through `Lights.AddZone`, `0x8004d9b0`), from where the map places it. Chunk scenery and plain scene objects get no light but the white ambient. The non-fullbright actors that maps place are nearly all torches (`0x186E`), whose meshes are unlit, so in practice map lights light the player. The user compared the fire in the Gwernia interiors' A01 in the original: Alaron is lit warm from the fireplace's side and the townsman beside him isn't, as in the port. Lighting other bodies too may be worth doing one day, but the port keeps the original's for now. A light that faces the camera looks the same in both too, by the user's screenshots of the school's entrance hall at Talewok (JundarInteriors A01, its light 62, the only such light outside EhudUnder).
- **Each light** (`WorldLights.AddNear`, `0x8004d384`), if it's active (gated as the chunk loads, and never again, `VoxelObjects.RefreshActive`, `0x80017af8`):
  1. An occludable one (header 0x1000, 246 of them) is skipped if a sphere of 0.25 swept from the light to the body's centre hits a triangle that faces it, see-through ones (0x200) aside (`Collision.SweepBlocked`, `0x800a42e8`; the port's `SpherePhysics.Sweep`). The original sweeps the body's chunk alone; the port sweeps the loaded chunks.
  2. One the body's centre is no nearer than its radius to, in 3D, is skipped. Otherwise the falloff is (radius − distance) / radius: straight-line, no square.
  3. The first time in a frame something reaches the light, its colour moves on (below) and is scaled by that falloff, in place, in the light. Every time, it's scaled again by the screen fade's level (`ScreenFade.Alpha`, `0x800da0c8`: 1 clear, 0 black) and added, shining from the light, or from the camera's eye for the 25 with header bit 0x1 (`Camera.Listener`, `0x800d829c`), whose distance is still from the light. So a second thing the light reaches in the same frame takes the first one's falloff, whatever its own distance, and the fade twice. That's the original's, and the port does the same.
- **The colour** moves on once a frame, by the frame's ticks, and only in a frame something is in range, so a light nobody is near holds its colour. The port steps it per tick. Each kind gives a share of the way from its first colour to its second, and the colour is the first plus the difference times the share, truncated toward zero, channel by channel (`PointLight.Blend`, `0x8004cf88`; `PointLight.Scale`, `0x8004d220`, is × the share, `ColorBytes.Single`, which the rules only call with 0–1):
  - *Static*: the first colour.
  - *Alternate*: the share goes up by ticks / (speed × 30). Past 1, the two colours change places and it wraps (`Lights.SwapColours`, `0x8004d17c`; `PointLight.Wrap`, `0x8004d150`, which takes off one period), so it fades there and back, speed × 30 ticks each way.
  - *Sine*: an angle goes up by 360 / (speed × 60) degrees a tick, wrapping at 360, and the share is (sin + 1) / 2: a cycle of speed × 60 ticks, from half way.
  - *Random*: the share moves toward a target by ticks / (speed × 60); within a step of it, it takes the target and draws another from 0 to 1 (`Rng.Float0To1`, `0x800933d4`, on the main stream; the port draws from the cosmetic stream, ADR 0006).
- **Dynamic lights** (`AllocDynamicLight`, `0x8004db1c`; `FreeDynamicLight`, `0x8004dbe0`; the manager at `0x800d8900`): 16 slots, each a light like a map object's built by `InitLight` (`0x8004ce80`), with no occlusion, and a life that counts down by each frame's ticks from the zone engine's frame (`DynamicLights.Step`, `0x8004dc74`), going out at 0. A new one goes in the slot numbered by how many are lit, not a free one: the original keeps a free list but never reads it, so after one goes out before a later slot's, the next new light takes the later one's place while it's lit. The port does the same. They go when the zone engine is freed (`FreeZoneEngine`); the port clears them when a teleport changes map set. In 1.0 only a trapped container's explosion adds any (`Containers.Explode`, `0x8000ffe4`): four fires where the container is, each drifting at random (speed 0.75) between dark red (0x60, 0, 0) and yellow within 5, the first for 600 ticks and the others for 60 (`Explosion.Fire`, `0x800174f8`), then a burst within 10 that fades from orange (0xFF, 0x80, 0) to black over its 120 ticks (alternate at speed 4, `Explosion.Burst`, `0x80016c00`). Spells, torches and scripts add none. The particles that come with the explosion aren't ported.
- **In cut-scenes** the stage's zone engine draws as trek's does, so the actors its maps place are lit by their chunk's lights (`RenderVoxelScenes`, `MapObjects.SetActorLights`), and the cut-scene's actors are bodies, drawn by `RenderPlayers` (`0x800140f0`, its first pass, bodies whose `+0x70a` is 0) and lit by `Bodies.SetLights`: the sun while the sky is outdoor, the moon and the dynamic lights. `+0x70a` is written only by `InitGameTrek` (`0x80020f40`, the player) and combat's party spawn (`0x8005c474`), and a cut-scene's bodies are cleared as they're made, so none of them, Alaron and his companions included, is the party's, and no light map object reaches them (`WorldLights.ForBody` skips its lights). The stage's own zone engine starts its dynamic lights empty and nothing in a cut-scene adds any, so in practice the actors have the sun alone. Lights that face the camera shine from the script camera's eye. `RenderZones` takes the camera's aim in place of the player, for the scene objects' draw distance and the grid cell whose objects it draws. The port lights the stage the same way (`Game.LightStage`, with the stage's own `WorldLights`, and `BodyView.Light`, which `WandererView` and `CutSceneActorView` share).
- **Nothing is drawn** for a light: no glow or flare. The flames are their own scene objects.

## A scene's own lights

A Borg5 can list Borg4 lights (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw` (`0x80097a90`) lights a scene by them unless the scene has its engine-lit bit (`+0x1f4` & 0x8000, which `Scene.ResetLights` sets on bodies and the actors maps place) or bit 0x80 (whose only setter, `0x8009df6c`, is never called). Each light's colour is its Borg4's times its transform's scale, channel by channel (`+0x28`–`+0x30`, red by X, green by Y, blue by Z), each scale clamped to 0–1 and the product to 1, × 255 and truncated, into both copies (`0x80097d54`); light *k* shines along the third row of transform 1 + *k*'s matrix, × 90 into bytes. The ambient is tinted the same way by transform 1 + its index, which is the scene's first model node. Animations drive these scales, so a cinematic's lights brighten, dim and change colour: Roog's naming (0x2568) is lit (0.909, 0.355, 0.142) of its grey, warm and dim, and the Shadow's first shot (0x23F6) starts nearly dark. The last is the ambient: with *n* lights it hands the RSP *n* − 1 directional lights (at least 1), so with one alone that's the ambient, beside a black light (`0x800e4748`). A scene with none gets the white ambient (`0x800e4738`) and the black light.

- **The RSP's side** (F3DEX2 2.08's lighting overlay, ROM `0x800024e8`): on each vertex load it turns each light's direction into the model's space by the matrix and normalises it, so the 90 only sets the bytes' rounding (about 0.6°); the vertex's normal bytes are used as they are. Each light adds its colour × the dot of the two, ≤ (127/128)², and the ambient adds in full, clamped at 255: `shade = min(255, A + Σ floor(C · max(0, n · L) / 16384))`. The port's unit vectors make each light about 2% brighter than that, which it leaves. Below a matrix scale of about 0.01 the normalisation loses precision and dims, which no cinematic reaches.
- **In the port**, the export puts each light transform's scale on its light node and its animation (importer 36), and the cinematic view tints each light and the ambient by it (`CinematicView.Tinted`).
- **In the world, never.** No chunk scene and none of the 387 Borg5s that scene objects place has a light, so scenery always gets the white ambient. 254 of the 1,402 scenes have lights, and 191 of the 203 actors' scenes are among them, but bodies and the actors maps place have the engine-lit bit, so theirs are ignored for the sky's and the point lights.
- **The spells' visuals** (their actors and lasting scenes in a battle) are drawn without the engine-lit bit too, so their own lights light them, as the port has it (`OwnLights`, which the cinematic view shares); their prim is the screen's fade (`Scene.SetTint`).
- **The pause menu's scroll** (`0x0E63`) is drawn without the engine-lit bit, so its own three lights light it: a dim blue (0.19, 0.18, 0.25) and a warm (0.83, 0.73, 0.62) directional light and a black ambient, as the port has it (`N64Material.Lighting.Own`, [ui](ui.md#opening-and-closing)).

## Draw distance

There are no tiers. Each scene object has a radius, and each of its one or two levels of detail its own distance (`renderProx`), and `RenderVoxelScenes` draws and fades each level every frame. `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.SceneObjectFade` ports it, and the runtime's `ChunkStreamer.Fade` applies it each frame. Chunks' own scenes, the terrain, aren't faded: they're drawn out to the far plane, 180, and fogged from about 105.

- **From the player, not the camera.** Every distance is from the player's centre, in 3D (with no player, from the camera's aim). Godot's visibility ranges measure from the camera, which is several units behind the player, so they can't be used.
- **An object** isn't drawn from its radius out. Around Gwernia the radius is the last level's distance, except for chests. A chest's object has radius 30 but its model is drawn to 10: a container's object lists the closed, opened and destroyed models, of which only the first is a level of detail. Most of the trees around the spawn are drawn near to 15 and far to 40 or 45, others to 40 and 70, and Oriana's path lights to 5 and 24.
- **A level of detail** is drawn from 2 short of the previous level's distance to its own. It fades out over its last unit, and fades in over its first. So as the player walks away, the next level fades in before this one fades out, and at any distance at most one is part-drawn. The last level fades out over its last unit and the object is gone, rather than popping.
- **The view.** On the ground, an object more than 37.5° off the camera's look fades out by 42.5°, linearly in the angle's cosine, measured from the camera to the object's position (the share as a float, × 255.0 in double, truncated: `0x8000c8cc`). At the follow camera's pitch, a 4:3 screen's corners are about 33° off the look, so it saves drawing what's behind and to the sides without being seen, except for a large object near the camera whose middle is out of view and whose edge isn't. The `DrawWhenNear` flag (0x4) skips this within half the object's radius. A wider window widens the cone by the tangent (`ViewCone.For`), so the fade stays as far outside the screen's edge as it does at 4:3. That's only what's drawn: Oriana's path lights go out in trek's tick by the original's cone whatever the window ([map-objects](map-objects.md#orianas-path-lights)).
- **Between the camera and the player.** An object that isn't `Solid` (flag 0x1), nearer the camera than the player and inside a cone from the player toward the camera, 0.35 of the way back wide, is drawn at 96 of 255 in the middle up to 255 at the cone's edge, whatever the look gave (the width is the distance × 0.35 in double, and the alpha is `trunc(across / width × 159) + 0x60`, truncated before the 0x60 is added: `0x8000c984`; the measure is `SceneObjectFade.Between`, `0x8000c430`). So bushes and small trees the camera looks through go see-through. Solid ones, including the trees and chests around the spawn, stay.
- **How a faded level is drawn.** Its alpha is the lesser of the view's and its distance's. It's the scene's prim alpha (`SetSceneColors`), so texel alpha times it. Under 255, the scene is drawn after everything else, blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8` in `Scene.SetRenderMode`), testing depth but not writing it. The runtime sets Godot's per-instance `transparency`, which does the same: the transparent pass, depth tested and not written. The N64 material multiplies its own alpha into it. Cut-outs, the foliage and bushes, blend too, since the faded render mode has no alpha test, so texel alpha fades with the rest. Godot draws an alpha-scissor material at full alpha wherever it passes, whatever the instance's transparency, so a faded model's cut-out surfaces switch to a blended copy of their material (`N64Material.Faded`) and back when it's whole. They blend in on-screen values (see [on-screen values](#on-screen-values)).
- **Further than the original** is a setting on the options menu's graphics page, Draw distance and Model detail ([ui](ui.md#options)) (`DrawDistance`; for one run, `--draw-distance <scale>` and `--detail nearest`). The scale multiplies each object's radius and each level's distance, and the fades keep their widths, except where an empty level gives way to one that draws something: there the object vanishes as the player nears it, and the data's own distance is kept at any scale, the user's choice, so Oriana's path lights fade out from 4 to 3 at every draw distance (`SceneObjectFade.Boundary`). Any later object built the same way gets the same. Model detail's Highest (`DrawDistance.NearestDetail`) is the port's own, and keeps the original's choice of level wherever that choice draws nothing. A level draws nothing when every draw of its scene is one the game skips, as the export skips them (`SceneModel.Empty`): the first level of Oriana's path lights (`0x1910`) is one, an untextured grey quad whose Borg2 (`0x190F`) has the hidden bit, and they're the only objects with an empty level. So Highest works out each level's alpha as the original does, and draws the nearest level that draws anything at the greatest of the alphas of the levels that draw something, and nothing else (`SceneObjectFade.Alphas`). Where the original fades between two levels with models, the nearest stays whole, so a tree shows its first level from 0 out to where its last ends, fading out over that last unit. Where it fades into an empty level, the nearest fades as the level it replaces does, so the path lights, drawn as their billboard, fade out from 4 to 3 as the player nears them, as 1.0's do, and are put out within 3. An object whose levels are all empty draws nothing, and one whose last level were empty would go out where its last level with a model ends. Two limits: the 3×3 chunks are loaded around the player's chunk, so an object can be as little as one chunk (100 in the overworld) away before its chunk is gone, and past about 105 the fog takes everything anyway. So about 1.4× is as far as it goes on the overworld's 70s without loading more chunks and moving the fog.

## Billboards

A Borg5 transform with flag 0x200 is a card the game turns to the camera (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.Billboard` ports the matrix, and the runtime's `Billboards` applies it.

- **What the game draws.** Only the transform's world position and its scale times the scene object's survive. The card rolls by the transform's `rot.z`, turns so its +Z points at the camera's position, not along the way it looks, and is scaled along the world's axes (`guAlignF`). So it tilts back as the camera looks down on it, and it can roll as the camera passes overhead.
- **In the runtime.** The export puts a facing node between the billboard's node and its card. The runtime makes the facing node top-level and, every frame, sets it to the billboard's world position, turned and scaled as the game does. It uses the camera's interpolated position, as the scenery is drawn from. Animations still move and scale the billboard's node and roll the card, and the facing node reads them.

## Scrolling textures

Water and fire scroll: the game shifts the texture's bitmap sideways each time a scene loads it (see [textures](../formats/textures.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.TextureScroll` ports the counter, and the runtime's `TextureScrolls` applies it.

- **The shader** takes how far the image has moved as a share of its width (`scroll`), and subtracts it from S after the material's own clamping or mirroring, then samples with repeat. That wraps the image round inside the texture as the game's shift does. It moves in whole texels, as the original's does, so it steps rather than glides.
- **Each copy of a scene has its own.** As in the original, each copy of a scene gets its own copy of each scrolling texture: the runtime gives it its own materials for them (`TextureScrolls.Attach`). Sharing one texture between scenes makes the sea scroll two to three times too fast; with a copy per scene it moves 2/3 of a texel a frame.
- **See-through ones.** 206 scenes draw a scrolling texture through a see-through tint, mostly water: Gwernia's sea (0x0EAB) at alpha 0.5, and the light from Gwernia Castle's windows (0x1577 in scene 0x161B, an RGBA16 with no transparent texels) at 0.1. The tint's alpha is the material's prim alpha, set as a `Vector4` (see the instance-uniform trap under [the N64 material](#the-n64-material)).
- **When it moves.** Once a frame at 20 fps (every 3 ticks, `Cadence.TextureFrameTicks`), each copy of a scene the original would draw loads its scrolling textures, which moves them on. A scene that isn't drawn keeps its place. 20 is the user's choice: the console's average, a middle between spots that chug (the fire and gore outside Gwernia, about 15 fps) and interiors that run well. The original draws the centre chunk's two scenes always, and a neighbour's when the camera's view crosses it (`ChunkView`, from `ChunkView.Drawn`, `0x8000d270` in 1.0) or the player's chunk's map has it drawn whatever the view (`MapRecord.DrawnNeighbours`). The view is a kite of five lines on the ground, 200 long, straight ahead and 42.5° either side. In the chunks it draws, a scene object's levels of detail scroll while they're drawn at all.
- **Neighbours drawn whatever the view.** `RenderZones` (`0x8000d574`) walks the eight neighbours in its table's order (`0x800CD700`: row and column −1 −1, +1 −1, −1 +1, +1 +1, then 0 −1, 0 +1, −1 0, +1 0) with a bit that starts at 1 and doubles, and draws a neighbour, its scene and its objects, when the view crosses it or that bit is set in byte 0x1B of the player's chunk's Borg9 (`Zones.PlayerMap`, `0x800d8288`). `RenderTransZones` (`0x8000dd44`) does the same for the second scenes, from a table of the same eight then the centre (`0x800CD83C`), whose bit has run out of the byte by then, so the centre's second scene is drawn only when the view crosses the centre chunk too. That always happens in chunks 100 across, and only those have second scenes, so it never shows. The import carries the byte as each map's `DrawnNeighbours`, named by compass (rows run east, columns south): 21 chunks of the Overworld set it, from A15 to M22, and one Borg9 no chunk list names; none of Gwernia's. I18 draws its four corners, I19 all but its north-west, south-west and north. Nothing else in 1.0 reads the byte or calls the view test, so wanderers, sounds and the rest don't depend on which chunks are drawn. The only other thing the choice drives: without the Expansion Pak, a neighbour that isn't drawn has its scenes freed when memory runs low (under `0x18000` free).
- **Where the original differs:** a copy of a scene starts from nothing when it's loaded, so neighbouring chunks' water is out of step in the original, by however far apart they were loaded. The runtime's copies also start together when their chunk loads, but it keeps each scene object's levels of detail for as long as the chunk is loaded. The original frees a level of detail out of range and loads it again later without the Expansion Pak (`NoExpPak_ClearSceneVoxelIndex`), which restarts its water; with it, that isn't checked.

## Particles

The engine's particles ([particles](../formats/particles.md)) are drawn last in the frame, after the deferred scene objects. `ParticlesView` draws what the core's `ParticleHead` drew on its last frame, in the runtime and the editor.

- **The square** (`ParticleHead.Draw`, `0x800a92fc`): four vertices at ±353.55 × 16, scaled by the particle's scale × 0.001 and drawn at 16 to the unit, so a square 0.7071 × its scale on a side, centred on it. It faces along the camera's look and stays upright: one matrix a frame from `guAlign` of the look with no roll (`Particles.DrawPrologue`, `0x800a8f74`), which turns +Z back along the look, the same for every particle, not toward each one. The runtime turns each square by the camera's right and up, which are the same for a camera that doesn't roll. A particle with its own matrix (flag 0x4, the weather's splashes) is turned by that instead.
- **The texture** covers the square once, clamped, with its last row at the top (the vertices' T runs from the height down to 0), so the image shows upside down; the flames are stored that way up. It's filtered bilinearly, in one cycle, so with no mipmap levels.
- **The colour** is the particle's, each channel × `255.0f`, truncated and wrapped to a byte (`ColorBytes.Single`; a channel a fade has pushed negative wraps, `0x800a9480`), after × the screen's brightness when that's under 1, so particles darken with fades, alpha too. It's the vertices' shade: the combiner is `TEXEL0 × SHADE` for colour and alpha (`G_CC_MODULATERGBA`, `0xFC121824 FF33FFFF`), so an intensity texture gives its intensity as both. In on-screen values, as the N64 material is.
- **The blend** is `G_RM_ZB_XLU_SURF` (`0x504A50`): `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, tested against the depth buffer and not written, with no fog, no culling and no lighting (geometry mode `0x200005`). Nothing is sorted: emitters draw in slot order and each one's particles newest first. The runtime draws each slot as one multimesh at a render priority of 100 and up, so after every other translucent draw, unsorted within it and in the original's order between slots.
- **Between frames.** The core steps particles at 30 fps, as the original at that rate; the view moves each one on from where the frame before drew it to where the last did over the next two ticks, so they glide. The view does this itself every frame Godot draws, so its multimeshes must stay out of Godot's physics interpolation: that blends each instance from where the last physics tick left the same index, and an index is another particle once one is born or dies, which draws splashes for a frame where others had been and hides them where they were, at any frame rate above the tick's.
- **Precipitation.** Rain and snow are two emitters of the same pool that the weather steers ([weather](weather.md#rain-and-snow), `WeatherParticles`), drawn by the same view: streaks and flakes facing the camera, and splashes laid flat on the ground by their own orientation. The original's are sparse and small, a few streaks 0.177 across on screen at once, so the options menu's Rain and snow setting can make each spawn several drops, with a pool to match and the splashes held to 1.5 times the original's. The F3 overlay counts the particles in use, and the drops and splashes the last frame drew, with how many were on screen.

## Normals

- **Unlit meshes have no normals that face up.** They're exported without `NORMAL`, as the N64 doesn't light them, and Godot generates its own. A decal's angle fade (`normal_fade`), which compares the decal with the surface's normal, hid the player's shadow even on level unlit ground, so those generated normals don't face up. Anything that reads unlit geometry's normals will need them exported, or the winding checked.

## Environment maps

A Borg2 display list with flag 0x1 has `G_TEXTURE_GEN` on: its texture coordinates come from its normals, for shiny metal such as the opening's door hinges. 31 lists in 30 models have it; 20 are lit and 11 unlit. Addresses are from 1.0 and the RSP microcode it carries (F3DEX2 fifo 2.08).

- **The look-at** is made afresh before each such list (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`, `0x80097a90`, `guLookAtReflect` at `0x800987f4`): from the scene's eye (`+0xF8`) at the node's world position, the translation row of its world matrix (`+0xF0` of the node's record, before the model's scale), with world up (0, 1, 0), into the RSP's look-at X and Y, in world space as the lights are. The look is eye − at, normalised; right is up × look, and the second axis look × right.
- **The scene's eye** is the translation row of its camera matrix (`+0xC8`), which `Borg5Scene.EvaluateNodes` (`0x80095518`) fills from the scene's own camera, transform 0, unless the scene has flag `0x40`. A cinematic's shot and the title screen's scenery keep their camera, so they look from its animated position, and the shading turns as the camera moves (`Cinematic.StartShot` sets only flag `0x10`). Trek's centre chunk's scene is given trek's camera (`SceneSetCameraLookAt`, `0x8009e1b4`, from `HandleZoneEngineFrame`). Neighbour chunks, scene objects and actors have flag `0x40` (`0x8009e444`), so they look from the world's origin, a direction that turns only as the node moves.
- **No eye at the node:** 1.0's `guLookAtReflectF` (`0x800b9b90`) doesn't guard its normalisation, and the game runs with the invalid-operation trap on (FPCSR `0x01000800`), so a list whose node sat on its eye would fault. The opening's door hinges sit exactly on the origin while the doors are shut, which is why the cinematic's eye can't be the origin.
- **The coordinates:** the normal after the model matrix, normalised, gives u = (1 + n · right) / 2 and v = (1 + n · up) / 2 across the whole texture (`G_TEXTURE` scales these lists by the texture's width and height, `W << 6` and `H << 6`, where others have `0x8000`). A normal facing up samples the texture's bottom row. The tile wraps or clamps as any list's does; the 20 lit ones all wrap.
- **Without lighting there's no texgen:** the microcode generates coordinates only in its lighting path, so the 11 unlit lists use their own S and T, at that scale, 32,768 units across the texture. The one list with flag 0x2 (`G_TEXTURE_GEN_LINEAR`) is unlit, so the linear mode is never seen.
- **In the port** the export keeps these lists' textures, and the N64 material makes the coordinates from an instance's eye (`N64Material.TexGen`, `SetEye`), the world's origin unless set. A cinematic's view sets its camera's position each picture, which is exact.

## What's left

- **The lens flare's test** for what hides the sun uses the collision, not the depth buffer, so foliage doesn't hide it.
- **The projected sky under cameras the follow camera never is.** Cut-scene cameras can look straight up, and a few of the original's shots do. Three things break there, none of them in trek:
  - *Sprites flip overhead.* A patch is kept upright against the horizon by a right-hand side level with the ground and square to its heading. The sun passes straight overhead at noon, and the moon at 09:00 and 21:00. There the game turns the azimuth half round so the object carries on across the sky, which turns the patch's right-hand side round too. The image turns 180° in one frame about its top-left corner, which is what's pinned. So the sun's disc jumps by its own size, and the moon's crescent turns over. Taking the right-hand side from the azimuth before it turns would keep the image fixed to the sky, as something that rises top-up sets top-down after passing overhead. The clouds reach 53° at most, and the stars are points, so it's only the sun and moon.
  - *The camera is rebuilt from its look.* `SkyScreen.Project` gets only the way the camera looks, and assumes it's level from side to side (no roll) and 45° high. Looking exactly straight up or down draws no sprites at all. A camera that rolls leaves the sky upright while the scenery tilts, and one that pitches over the top draws the sky upside down against the scenery. A camera with another field of view, such as a cinematic shot's, unpins the sky from the scenery. The fix is to hand the core the camera's basis and field of view from Godot, not its look alone.
  - *The gradient's dome* is drawn from the camera's own basis and field of view, so it keeps up with any camera, but its top colour fills everything from 17° up: a cut-scene looking up sees a plain sky, where the original's squeezes the whole gradient onto the screen.
- **Texture filtering:** probably not worth emulating. Aidyn doesn't use the N64's three-point filtering for artistic effect, and its textures, especially the faces, have far more texels than the screen shows.
- **Animated textures.** No Borg5 uses the scene-level kind, which the game would crash on. The Borg1s that scroll are ported (see [scrolling textures](#scrolling-textures)).
- **Which scenes are drawn**, apart from how fast textures scroll. Two parts of the original's choice aren't ported:
  - *Its collision grid lists the scene objects to draw* from each square: the player's in the centre chunk, and the squares along a neighbour's edge. The runtime draws every object within its range.
  - *It draws a neighbour chunk only when the camera's view crosses it* or the player's chunk's map has it drawn, objects and all. The runtime draws all nine, and leaves what's out of view to Godot. Only the scroll counts the original's way (`ChunkStreamerView.DrawnScenes`).
- **Billboards in the editor's model view** face the way they were modelled. Its map set view turns them, as the runtime does.
- **Opened and destroyed containers in the editor.** Once a chest, sack, crate or barrel is opened or destroyed, the original swaps its linked scene object's model for the object's second or third (`replace_container_voxel`). The runtime does ([map-objects](map-objects.md#a-chunks-objects-at-run-time)); the editor's **Flags** switch only leaves the container itself out.
- **Environment maps in trek.** 16 scenes have lit environment-mapped lists: two cinematic shots, five chunk scenes, four scene objects' and three actors'. The port looks at trek's from its world's origin, a map set's corner, where the original's is probably the centre chunk's, and the centre chunk's own scene should look from trek's camera ([environment maps](#environment-maps)). Settling the original's world frame for drawing would make them exact.
- **The particles' first pass** (emitter flag 0x1000: after the centre chunk's scene, without the depth buffer, comparing the primitive's depth) isn't drawn apart; nothing the port runs has it.
- **A scene's own lights in the other scenes that have them.** The pause menu's scroll, the cinematics' shots and the spells' visuals use their Borg4 lights in the port ([a scene's own lights](#a-scenes-own-lights)). The rest that have lights are actors' scenes, which ignore them.
