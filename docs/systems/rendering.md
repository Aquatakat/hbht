# Rendering

How the runtime draws scenes as the N64 does, and what's left. The runtime's other stand-ins are in [runtime](runtime.md).

## On-screen values

The N64 has no linear light. Its texels, vertex colours, tints and blends are the values it puts on screen, and the game turns the VI's gamma off (`osViSetSpecialFeatures` in `gfx.cpp`), so the emulator shows the framebuffer as it is. Godot works in linear light, where the same numbers come out differently:

- **Multiplying.** A texel times a vertex shade of 0.5 is half as bright on the N64. Treated as linear, the shade shows at about 73%. The scene export used to write vertex colours as they are into `COLOR_0`, which glTF defines as linear, so baked shading looked washed out: the birch at the start of a new game came out twice as bright as the original's.
- **Blending.** Godot blends in linear light. A surface at 10% over dark stone adds far more than 10% of its brightness there, and layers of it build up fast: the light from Gwernia Castle's windows (0x1577 at alpha 0.1) came out as harsh white sheets where the N64's are faint (the user's comparison, Oct 4, 2026). For black over the scene, the same alpha darkens the screen about half as much: the player's shadow, 31% on the N64, looked like 15%.

The export now decodes vertex colours and untextured tints as sRGB, so they're correct glTF and a viewer that multiplies in linear light comes out almost as the N64 does (a power curve carries through a product, and sRGB is nearly one). The N64 material goes further and works in on-screen values itself.

**The runtime's 3D image holds on-screen values,** so Godot's blender mixes them as the N64's does. The N64 material writes its on-screen colour without turning it linear (`N64Material.OnScreenFramebuffer`), and the environment's colour correction is a table that decodes sRGB (`OnScreenOutput`). Godot applies colour correction after it encodes the image for the screen, so the two cancel and the screen gets the values as drawn. The sky's backdrop is a canvas that Godot decodes as it takes it in behind the scene, so a full-screen pass at the top of its layer encodes it first. The canvas has 8 bits a channel, so near white, neighbouring shades of the sky can merge. Checked against the code before (Oct 4, 2026): opaque scenery and the sky came out within one level of 255; the floor under the castle's light went from 67.6 to 52.8; and the shadow, now at its own alpha, darkens the ground to 0.68 of itself at its middle, the N64's 1 − 80/255. Everything translucent blends this way: water, the faded levels of detail, fire and the shadow. The editor still works in linear light, since Godot's lights light it.

## The N64 material

`N64Material` (in the runtime, linked into the Inspector) replaces every material Godot's glTF import makes, in the runtime and in the Inspector's previews. It builds each from the glTF's own material, because Godot's import converts colours and samplers in ways the N64 doesn't.

- **The combiner.** `setStaticMode` sets textured draws to `TEXEL0 × SHADE`, then `× PRIMITIVE` in the second cycle, and untextured ones to `SHADE × PRIMITIVE` (combine modes `0x127E03, 0xFF0FF3FF` and `0xFFFE03, 0xFF0E79FF`, decoded by hand). Alpha is texel alpha times prim alpha, or shade alpha times prim alpha: a textured draw ignores its vertices' alpha. The shader reads raw texels (no `source_color`), turns the vertex colour back into on-screen values and multiplies there. In the runtime it hands Godot the on-screen result (see [on-screen values](#on-screen-values)), and in the editor the linear one.
- **The blender** (`Scene.SetRenderMode`, `0x80097618`, which sets every scene draw's render mode and prim colour). Prim alpha is the scene's alpha × (1 − the mesh tint's alpha) × (1 − the model's alpha), truncated to a byte: the castle's light at 0.1 is 25, Gwernia's sea at 0.5 is 127. The draw is skipped when either alpha of the model or mesh is 1. Under 255 it's blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8`): an ordinary blend, `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, depth tested and not written, whatever the texture. At 255, a texture with transparent texels (Borg1 flag 0x20) is blended if it's IA16, IA8 or RGBA32 and cut out otherwise (`G_RM_AA_ZB_TEX_EDGE2`, `0x113078`, coverage times texel alpha), and everything else is opaque (`G_RM_AA_ZB_OPA_SURF2`, `0x112078`). There's no alpha compare anywhere (`G_AC_NONE`, and no `G_SETBLENDCOLOR`), so the cut-outs' edge is the coverage's, which the port's 0.5 threshold stands in for. Every scene starts from the static state list at `0x800E4758` (`Scene.InitDrawState`, `0x80097300`), and each display list adds its own (`SetStaticMode`, `0x800973b8`). Checked against 1.0's machine code by a research run (Oct 4, 2026); the render-mode constants spot-checked.
- **The original's dither.** Translucent draws on the emulator look grainy. That's the RDP's colour dither (`G_CD_MAGICSQ`, a fixed 4 × 4 pattern) as it writes each pixel to the 16-bit framebuffer (5 bits a channel; `StartGfxList` sets it), with the VI's dither filter on (`osViSetSpecialFeatures(0x62)`: gamma off, divot off, dither filter on). The port draws at full precision and doesn't dither, so its blends are smooth.
- **Order.** The original draws a scene's display lists in its instructions' order, translucent ones where they fall, and sorts nothing. Godot draws translucent surfaces after opaque ones, sorted by distance, which can differ where two translucent surfaces overlap. The castle's light is the second-to-last draw of its scene, after all the opaque ones, so it's the same there. Across scenes, the original draws a frame in this order (`RenderZones`, `0x8000d574`, and `HandleZoneEngineFrame`, from their labels): the sky; each neighbour chunk in view, its scene and then its objects; the centre chunk's scene; the particles of emitters flagged for the first pass, which none of the scenes' are; the bodies' shadows; bodies in water; the centre chunk's objects; translucent bodies in water; the other bodies; the chunks' second scenes, such as water (`RenderTransZones`, `0x8000dd44`); then the scene objects that are fading or flagged `Deferred` (`Zones.DrawDeferredScenes`, `0x8000dc98`); then every other emitter's particles (`ProcessAndRenderParticleHead`, `0x800a842c`, from `HandleZoneEngineFrame`), and the weather.
- **Shade** is the vertex colour on unlit display lists. On lit ones it's the RSP's lighting (see [bodies' light](#bodies-light) below), which the runtime works out per vertex (`N64Material.RspLighting`). The Inspector still lets Godot's lights stand in (Lambert diffuse, no specular).
- **Prim** is the scene's tint, `SceneData.colorFloats`, times the mesh tint for untextured draws (`Scene.SetRenderMode`, `FUN_800a1184` on the debug build). Every chunk scene and scene object gets `gMainColor` (`SetSceneColors`), which the sky sets (below). A scene object with the `Tint` flag and a non-zero tint gets the average of the two instead: 1,398 objects have it. In 1.0's machine code (`Zones.SetSceneColors`, `0x8000c298`), `RenderVoxelScenes` passes the object's tint word (+0x64) only when the flag is set, and when the whole word, alpha and all, isn't 0, each channel is `(gMainColor + tint) >> 1`, an even average truncated, with nothing clamped or weighted. That's then scaled by the screen fade's brightness (`0x8004d220` with `ScreenFade.Alpha`, 1 when nothing fades), which the untinted path doesn't do. I08, where a new game starts, has 15 of Oriana's path lights tinted a pale cyan (153, 236, 255), and M07 has a birch tinted grey (88, 85, 86), which comes out at about 70% of its neighbours at noon. Bodies get white and are lit instead (`some_player_render_sub`, `set_sun_light`), so actors' meshes are marked as bodies.
- **The tint went in linear.** `N64Material.SetObjectTint` gave the instance uniform a Godot `Color`, which Godot turns linear for an instance uniform, so the path lights' tint came out as (81, 214, 255) and their red a fifth short: the user found them bluer than the N64's (Sep 29, 2026). It's a `Vector4` now, as prim is. Checked against the user's capture `roms/pathlight.png`, which the sundial puts at 18:40 (its glow 200° round the ring): a light's billboard (`0x374A`, a white IA texture with texel alpha up to 210, vertex colours white, blended as any transparent texture is, nothing additive) peaks there at (129–136, 165–169, 168–177), its 50 brightest pixels averaging (128.9, 164.5, 169.0). The code predicts prim (154, 189, 199) from 18:40's `gMainColor` (156, 142, 144), so (132, 160, 166) at alpha 210 over the dark forest floor. The runtime at the same time, free camera 8 from light 104 (`--fly 906.6,-18.5,852 --time 18.67 --flags 50`), averaged (104.4, 150.9, 166.1) before the fix and (133.5, 159.9, 166.1) after. What's left of the difference is the N64's 16-bit framebuffer and its dither, and a greener background.
- **Fog** is per vertex, as the RSP computes it. The amount is the depth buffer's `z / w` for the original's planes, on the 0–1000 scale `gSPFogPosition` takes (0 is `z / w` of −1, 1000 is 1), between the fog's start and end. Draws blend toward the fog colour in on-screen values before the pixel is written, as `G_RM_FOG_SHADE_A` does. In the editor, where Godot's lights light the lit ones, those use Godot's `FOG` output, after its lighting, in linear light. With trek's planes of 1 and 180 and no fog in the weather, the fog runs from 996 to 1000, which is from about 105 units to the far plane. The weather's fog moves the start in to 996 − trunc(`fogTime` × 40), as far as 966 (`Zones.SetSceneColors`, `0x8000c298`; [weather](weather.md#the-fog)), except while a cut-scene plays. The runtime's follow camera now stops at 180, as the original's does.
- **Wrapping.** Borg2 materials clamp, repeat or mirror S and T separately (faces are often half a texture, mirrored), which a `BaseMaterial3D` can't, so the shader wraps each axis itself, as `WrapMaterials` used to for the materials that needed it.
- **Globals.** The tint, the fog colour and the fog's range are global shader parameters (`n64_tint`, `n64_fog_color`, `n64_fog`), untinted and unfogged until the runtime sets them. The Inspector leaves them so.

Measured at the spawn, against the code before the material, at the same spot and camera: the birch trunk went from 87 to 47 (0.54×) and the ground from 52 to 31, while Alaron, who is lit, didn't change. The noon tint then takes them to 41 and 27. The original's screenshot of the same birch peaks at about (72–80, 75–83, 60–68), and ours at noon at (77, 80, 66).

## The sky's colours

Every frame, `Sky::RenderSky` sets five colours from the sky gradient of the terrain the player is in, and the scenery is multiplied by the first. `Aidyn.Core.Sky` ports it, and the runtime's `SkyView` applies it each tick.

- **The gradients** are eight Borg8s, 12 × 37 RGBA32 (`sky_borg8s`, read from the code segment). Rows 0–31 are the sky from top to bottom. Rows 32–36 are the tint, sky, fog, cloud and sun colours. There's a column every two hours from midnight, and `Sky::GenerateSky` blends between the two columns either side of the time of day, wrapping from 22:00 to midnight.
- **The tint** (`gMainColor`) is `tint row × 0.65 + 0.15`, plus a fifth of the sky colour, clamped and truncated to bytes (`Sky::SetColors`; the constants are checked against the debug ROM's machine code). The tint row is grey: 88 at midnight, 225 at 10:00 and noon. So at a new game's noon on terrain 2, the scenery is multiplied by (222, 225, 227). Lens flare darkens it by half its value while the camera faces the sun (see [the sky's objects](#the-skys-objects)).
- **Checked against the emulator** at the spawn at about 04:00 and 20:00. Between the user's two shots every region, from backdrop to leaves, changed by the same (0.75, 0.84, 0.84), so the time of day is one multiplier over the scenery, as ported. The clear sky predicts (0.78, 0.86, 0.87) from 04:00 to 20:00 and (0.75, 0.84, 0.84) to 20:20; the runtime's own renders changed by (0.78, 0.85, 0.86). The user thought it might be raining at 20:00. The rain sky (sky 2) at about 18:40 would fit as well, since both tints are nearly grey, so these shots don't show whether rain changes the sky. At 04:00, with the views matched, ours over the original's was even across R, G and B in each region: the warm dawn tint is right. The brightness matched at the foot of the backdrop and was about 10% dark on the ground. Upper areas showed different trees in the two views, so they don't count.
- **Which sky.** `BorgMaps::GetMapTerrain` picks a sky number by terrain in a switch, which the importer carries as a fact (checked against the debug ROM's jump table) on terrain records. Terrain types 6, 8, 9, 11 and 13–16 don't draw the gradient: the background is black, there's no weather, but the colours still come from it. Every new chunk the player enters chooses again (`Sky::SetBackgroundType` with 600), which fades to a different sky over 600 ticks. Choosing again restarts the fade, and choosing the sky that's up abandons it. Into or out of a sky that isn't drawn, between two that aren't, and as trek's world is built, the sky changes at once (`LoadGameBorgScenes`, `0x8000b05c`, `SkyState.Enter`). Rain and snow bring the rain sky and hold it against entering chunks ([weather](weather.md#the-rain-sky)).
- **The clock.** A new game starts at noon on day 2 of week 6 (`InitGameGlobals`), and trek time runs 72 times as fast as real time, so a day takes 20 minutes. `--time <hours>` starts at another time, and the overlay shows it.
- **The backdrop.** Behind the scenery, `Sky::GenerateGradient` draws rows 0–31 of the gradient down the screen, a line at a time, on the original's 320 × 240 screen (`GetVRes` is always 240). `SkyBackdrop` ports it and `SkyBackdropView` draws it, on a canvas layer the environment shows as the background, scaled to the window's height. The gradient spans 200 lines, from row 1/200 of the way down at the top of the screen to its foot at line 200 when the camera looks level. Looking down moves it up by sin(y) × 240 lines, where y is the height of the look's unit vector, so the sine of a sine: 61 lines at the follow camera's usual 15°. Looking up doesn't move it down. Below the foot the screen takes the foot's colour, and looking down so far that no line is left (sin(y) × 240 over 200) leaves it black. It isn't projected, so it only roughly meets the scenery's horizon, and the far plane hides the difference. Under `SkyPlacement.Projected`, the default, the runtime projects it as a dome instead (`SkyBackdrop.DomeFraction`, drawn per pixel by a shader in `SkyBackdropView`): each pixel takes the gradient's line for the elevation of the way through it, so its rows stay fixed against the scenery, as the sun and clouds do. Each elevation keeps the line the original gives it down the middle of the screen when the follow camera looks down as it usually does: its 15° raises it by sin 15° of its distance, so it looks down 14.5° and the original moves the gradient up 59 lines. Then the horizon is 0.52 of the way down the gradient, its top is 17.1° up, above which the sky is its top colour, and its foot 18.5° under the horizon, behind the ground. At that pitch the two placements agree within a level or two (screenshots over the sea at 15° down, at noon, clear and raining), and in fog both are the fog colour. They part as the camera pitches: looking level, the original's pale foot sits at the scenery's edge, 28 levels brighter there than the dome's, which keeps it under the horizon as at the usual pitch; looking 15° up, the original still squeezes the whole gradient onto the screen, pale band and all, floating over a horizon that's off the bottom, where the dome fills the upper sky with its top colour; looking 30° down, the original's gradient has moved up less than the horizon (115 lines against 167), and the dome keeps the pale band at the horizon, 37 levels brighter at the top of the screen. The follow camera keeps to 0°–45° of pitch, orbiting, so it's the dome's way with the projected sky's objects that earns it its place: the sky no longer slides against the scenery and the sun as the camera pitches. `--sky original` or F6 draws the original's. The weather's fog fades it to the fog colour, fully from half thick, lens flare lifts it, and lightning fills the screen white in its place for a frame ([weather](weather.md#lightning-and-thunder)). Checked against the runtime's screenshot: the lines come out as computed, within a byte or two of the follow camera's pitch, so the canvas passes on-screen values through.

## The sky's objects

An outdoor sky draws, in order: the gradient, then the stars, the sun and the moon (`Skyobjects::Render`), then the clouds (`Clouds::Render`), then the scenery, then the lens flare (`Lensflare::Render`), then the HUD. The tables come from the ROM on the sky record, `aidyn:sky/0` (see the [importer](importer.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Sky` has `SkyObjects`, `Clouds` and `LensFlare`; `SkyBackdropView` draws the first two behind the scene and `LensFlareView` the flare over it. All are sprites on the 320 × 240 screen, texel × colour with texel alpha × alpha, blended normally (`borg8DlistInit` with flag 6: combine `0xFC119623 FF2FFFFF`, `TEXEL0 × PRIMITIVE` for both, and `G_RM_XLU_SURF2`), with bilinear filtering. Godot's 2D blends in on-screen values too.

- **Placement isn't projected.** Each object has two angles, a height and an azimuth. From the camera's look, x is 160 plus 300 × the sine of the azimuth against the look's heading, and y is 120 plus 300 × the sine of the elevation against the look's height. Perspective puts the scenery at 289.7 × the tangent instead (120 / tan 22.5°, for the Borg3 cameras' 45°). The two agree in the middle of the screen, but toward its sides the scenery turns up to about 30% faster than the sky, so the clouds, sun, moon and stars drag along with the camera. The user saw the same stretching toward the screen's edges in the emulator, only less of it, since 4:3 shows less edge than 16:9. By default the runtime goes past the original here (`SkyPlacement.Projected`; `--sky original` or F6 for the original's). It gives each sprite a direction and projects it as the scenery is projected (`SkyScreen.Project`: a 45° pinhole, pitch and all), so the sky stays pinned against the scenery at any pitch, like a skybox. An object's direction is its azimuth and elevation. The follow camera looks 15° down, so projecting only across, at the tangent, wasn't enough: a point above the horizon also sits higher toward the sides, by 1 / cos of its angle off the look. Each sprite is a patch of sky, not only a point. It's laid flat, facing the camera, a unit away in its direction, and kept upright against the horizon. It's sized so it's drawn as the original draws it when the camera looks straight at it (`SkyPatch`). Its corners are projected like the scenery, and it's drawn as a 4 × 4 grid of triangles, so the texture follows the perspective. Placing the sprites only by a point wasn't enough. Perspective spreads points apart toward the screen's edges, by up to 1 / cos² of the angle off the look, while a fixed-size rectangle doesn't grow with them, so clouds that touched in the middle drifted apart at the sides (the user's playtest, Sep 29, 2026). As patches, sprites stretch and tilt toward the edges as the scenery does, and keep their places against each other. The sky also reads the camera's interpolated transform, as the scenery is drawn from, not the last tick's. The azimuth turns half round while the height is between 90° and 270°, so an object carries on over the top. The elevation is sin(height) × 90°, not the height, so the sun is drawn at about 64° at 09:00 while it lights from 45°. The sprite's top-left corner goes at the point, not its middle.
- **The sun**'s height is the time of day × 360° − 90°, so it's on the horizon at 06:00 and 18:00, and its azimuth is 90°: it rises in +X, as its light does. It's drawn in the sky's sun colour at alpha 250, at scale 1 (64 pixels). Where its middle lands is kept for the lens flare, and kept while it isn't drawn.
- **The moon**'s images are IA8 with every intensity at full, so each is a white disc shaped by its alpha, with no surface. Its height is twice (time of day × 360° + 90°), so it goes round twice a day, and it sways 40° either side of its azimuth of 0. Its image is its phase (`World::set_moonPhase`) on a cycle of four weeks, with the first two counted as waxing, though the images show it waning then: full on days 0–2, a quarter on 3–7, a crescent on 9–13, new on 14–16, a crescent on 17–21 and a quarter on 23–27, with days 8 and 22 turning at noon. A new game starts under a new moon (week 6, day 2: day 16), whose image is faint (alpha at most 85).
- **The stars**, 91 of them, show only while the sun is down, from 18:00 to 06:00, at the sine of how far down it is × their alpha, so they come out as it sets. Each frame each takes a random grey from 160 to 255, so they twinkle. `Skyobjects::Init` sets their scale to 0.19 over the table's 0.2. One star has alpha 0 in the ROM and never shows.
- **The clouds**, 55 of them, are points on a plane within 750 of the camera, drawn in the sky's cloud colour. A cloud's nearness is 1 − distance / 750, and it's drawn 240 × nearness above the horizon line (120 + 300 × the look's height), at nearness + 0.2 times its size, with alpha × √nearness; it's centred by nearness, not by its scale, so slightly off. Projected, a cloud is a direction: its heading on the plane, at the elevation the original draws it at when the camera looks straight at it, where the original's sines and perspective agree. 240 × nearness pixels at 300 to the unit is a sine of 0.8 × nearness, from the horizon at the edge of the plane to 53° up over the camera. The plane goes with the camera, so no distance is involved: walking and turning don't move a cloud against the scenery, only the wind does. It's a dome at infinity. Pinning clouds to the world, with parallax, would take a cloud deck at a height, which spreads them differently over the sky from the original's even steps of nearness. Real clouds 1 to 2 km up would barely shift at walking pace anyway (the scenes' units are about metres). The game takes the way to the camera with X and Z swapped against the sky objects' azimuths, which only mirrors the layout. The wind blows them 4 × its velocity a frame, which the port spreads over the frame's ticks, and one that goes past 750 comes back in on the far side. A new game's wind is (1, −0.1, 1) normalised at 15/648 a tick, and it drifts on its own, whatever the weather (`Weather.DriftWind`, [weather](weather.md#the-wind)). They fade in and out as the cloud cover passes each one's threshold, but nothing changes the cover from 1, so all show.
- **The weather's fog** fades the sun by its thickness, and the moon, stars and clouds faster (× 1.5, 2 and 1.5), as the [weather](weather.md) has it.
- **The lens flare** shows while the sun's middle is on screen, 10 pixels inside the edges, and nothing hides it. It fades in by 10 a frame up to 50 and out the same, over 5 frames. Its eight pieces are drawn along the line from the middle of the screen through the sun, at their distance along it, at their scale × the sun's distance from the middle / 80 up to 1, and not at all at 0.2 or less, in their colours at alpha fade × the tint's red. Within 80 of the middle it also sets `lensFlareVal`, the tint's red × (1 − distance / 80) × 0.5 × fade / 50, which next frame lowers the tint by half of it and lifts the gradient by all of it. The original tests the depth buffer under the sun's middle at the end of each frame, for the next. The port sends a ray through that point against the collision, out to the far plane, which catches the ground and buildings but not leaves.

## Bodies' light

The sky lights bodies and animated scene objects; scenery it only tints. `Aidyn.Core.Sky.SkyLight` has the pieces, and the N64 material lights each vertex from global parameters that `SkyView` sets each tick.

- **The RSP's lighting** is the ambient plus each light's colour times the cosine between the vertex's normal and the way to the light, where that's positive, clamped to 1, in on-screen values, per vertex. The game puts the camera in the projection matrix (`gsAnimationDataMtx` loads it with `G_MTX_PROJECTION`), so the modelview is the model alone and the lights are in world space.
- **Three kinds of lit mesh** (`N64Material.Lighting`). Bodies, the player among them, get white prim, an ambient of half `gSkyColor` truncated to bytes, and the sun (`some_player_render_sub`). Scene objects' actors get white prim, `gMainColor` as ambient, and the sun (`set_sun_light`), unless they're fullbright, when they're tinted like scenery. Scenery's lit meshes keep the white ambient `Scene::SetLightData` gives every scene and no other light, so they come out as the tint alone. Bodies and actors also get the [point lights](#point-lights) that reach them.
- **The sun** is `gSunColor` from the direction `Skyobjects::UpdateSun` works out: at an angle of the time of day × 360° − 90° about the Z axis, turned by the sun's 90° in the sky objects' table. It rises in +X at 06:00, is overhead at noon and sets in −X at 18:00, and lights from below at night, when the gradient's sun colour is dark. Only an outdoor sky has it; in caves (`SkyType4`) bodies get the ambient alone.
- **Which copy of the colour.** `Scene::addLight` writes the colour into `colc`, the second of the two copies in `Light_t`, and zeroes `col` (bytes 0–3 zero, 4–6 the colour and 8–10 the direction, × 120, in the debug ROM's machine code; the decomp's `dir[1]` twice is a typo there). The sun does light Alaron on the emulator: in the user's daylight capture the brightest parts of his shirt are about 216, and the ambient alone can give at most 127. So F3DEX2 reads `colc`, and the port takes the colour as the game means it.
- **The moon** adds a light only when `playerData.tintScale` is non-zero, which only combat's tints change, so in trek it's black. Not ported.
- **Point lights** add more: light map objects and dynamic lights (below), as directional lights from the body toward each.

## Point lights

Light map objects (777 in the base game) and the game's dynamic lights light bodies and the actors maps place, as directional lights worked out for each draw. `Aidyn.Core.Lights` ports them (`PointLight`, `WorldLights`, `SceneLights`, `DynamicLights`), and the runtime's `Game.LightBodies` hands each lit model its lights every tick, which the N64 material adds to the sun and ambient per vertex (`N64Material.SetLights`, up to four). Settled from 1.0's machine code by a research run (Oct 17, 2026), with the room for lights, the falloff and the colour formulas checked in the disassembly.

- **How many.** A scene has room for 7 lights and an ambient (`Scene.ResetLights`, `0x8009e778`). `SceneSetMaxDynamicDirLights` (`0x8009e7d0`) lowers it to 4 for bodies (`RenderPlayers`, `0x800140f0`) and 2 for actors a map places (`RenderVoxelScenes`, `0x8000cd3c`); nothing else calls it. The sun comes first while the sky is outdoor, then the moon, so a body outdoors has 3 for point lights and an actor 1. Once the room is full, a new light takes the place of the dimmest one after the sun and moon by r² + g² + b² (`Scene.LightBrightness`, `0x8009e800`) if that's dimmer, and is dropped otherwise (`SceneLights.Add`, `0x8009e838`, which ignores the brightness byte it's passed). The colour goes in the second copy, `colc`, and the direction × 120 (see above). The draw (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`) hands the RSP the lights and the ambient, and empties the list.
- **What's lit.** A body (`Bodies.SetLights`, `0x80013798`) gets the sun, the moon, the dynamic lights (`Lights.AddDynamic`, `0x8004d9e0`), and then, only if it's one of the party's (`+0x70a`, which `InitGameTrek` sets on the player and combat on the party's members, not their enemies), the light map objects that the grid cell under its centre lists in its own chunk (`WorldLights.ForBody`, `0x8004d878`). So in trek, only the player is lit by map lights; wanderers get the dynamic lights alone. An actor a map places that isn't fullbright gets the sun, the dynamic lights and every light map object of its chunk (`MapObjects.SetActorLights`, `0x80013ac4`, through `Lights.AddZone`, `0x8004d9b0`), from where the map places it. Chunk scenery and plain scene objects get no light but the white ambient. Of the 90 actors maps place that aren't fullbright, 73 stand within a light, but nearly all are torches (`0x186E`, 69 of them), whose meshes are unlit, so in practice map lights light the player.
- **Each light** (`WorldLights.AddNear`, `0x8004d384`), if it's active (gated as the chunk loads, and never again, `VoxelObjects.RefreshActive`, `0x80017af8`):
  1. An occludable one (header 0x1000, 246 of them) is skipped if a sphere of 0.25 swept from the light to the body's centre hits a triangle that faces it, see-through ones (0x200) aside (`Collision.SweepBlocked`, `0x800a42e8`; the port's `SpherePhysics.Sweep`). The original sweeps the body's chunk alone; the port sweeps the loaded chunks.
  2. One the body's centre is no nearer than its radius to, in 3D, is skipped. Otherwise the falloff is (radius − distance) / radius: straight-line, no square.
  3. The first time in a frame something reaches the light, its colour moves on (below) and is scaled by that falloff, in place, in the light. Every time, it's scaled again by the screen fade's level (`ScreenFade.Alpha`, `0x800da0c8`: 1 clear, 0 black) and added, shining from the light, or from the camera's eye for the 25 with header bit 0x1 (`Camera.Listener`, `0x800d829c`), whose distance is still from the light. So a second thing the light reaches in the same frame takes the first one's falloff, whatever its own distance, and the fade twice. That's the original's, and the port does the same; with the player alone in range of most lights, it seldom shows.
- **The colour** moves on once a frame, by the frame's ticks, and only in a frame something is in range, so a light nobody is near holds its colour. The port steps it per tick. Each kind gives a share of the way from its first colour to its second, and the colour is the first plus the difference times the share, truncated toward zero, channel by channel (`PointLight.Blend`, `0x8004cf88`):
  - *Static*: the first colour.
  - *Alternate*: the share goes up by ticks / (speed × 30). Past 1, the two colours change places and it wraps (`Lights.SwapColours`, `0x8004d17c`; `PointLight.Wrap`, `0x8004d150`, which takes off one period), so it fades there and back, speed × 30 ticks each way.
  - *Sine*: an angle goes up by 360 / (speed × 60) degrees a tick, wrapping at 360, and the share is (sin + 1) / 2: a cycle of speed × 60 ticks, from half way.
  - *Random*: the share moves toward a target by ticks / (speed × 60); within a step of it, it takes the target and draws another from 0 to 1 (`Rng.Float0To1`, `0x800933d4`, on the main stream; the port draws from the cosmetic stream, ADR 0006).
- **Dynamic lights** (`AllocDynamicLight`, `0x8004db1c`; `FreeDynamicLight`, `0x8004dbe0`; the manager at `0x800d8900`): 16 slots, each a light like a map object's built by `InitLight` (`0x8004ce80`), with no occlusion, and a life that counts down by each frame's ticks from the zone engine's frame (`DynamicLights.Step`, `0x8004dc74`), going out at 0. A new one goes in the slot numbered by how many are lit, not a free one: the original keeps a free list but never reads it, so after one goes out before a later slot's, the next new light takes the later one's place while it's lit. The port does the same. They go when the zone engine is freed (`FreeZoneEngine`); the port clears them when a teleport changes map set. In 1.0 only a trapped container's explosion adds any (`Containers.Explode`, `0x8000ffe4`): four fires where the container is, each drifting at random (speed 0.75) between dark red (0x60, 0, 0) and yellow within 5, the first for 600 ticks and the others for 60 (`Explosion.Fire`, `0x800174f8`), then a burst within 10 that fades from orange (0xFF, 0x80, 0) to black over its 120 ticks (alternate at speed 4, `Explosion.Burst`, `0x80016c00`). Spells, torches and scripts add none. The particles that come with them aren't ported.
- **In cut-scenes** (read from 1.0's machine code, Oct 17, 2026): the stage's zone engine draws as trek's does, so the actors its maps place are lit by their chunk's lights (`RenderVoxelScenes`, `MapObjects.SetActorLights`), and the cut-scene's actors are bodies, drawn by `RenderPlayers` (`0x800140f0`, its first pass, bodies whose `+0x70a` is 0) and lit by `Bodies.SetLights`: the sun while the sky is outdoor, the moon and the dynamic lights. `+0x70a` is written only by `InitGameTrek` (`0x80020f40`, the player) and combat's party spawn (`0x8005c474`), and a cut-scene's bodies are cleared as they're made, so none of them, Alaron and his companions included, is the party's, and no light map object reaches them (`WorldLights.ForBody` skips its lights). The stage's own zone engine starts its dynamic lights empty, and nothing in a cut-scene adds any, so in practice the actors have the sun alone. Lights that face the camera shine from the script camera's eye. Where the stage is drawn from differs: `RenderZones` takes the camera's aim in place of the player, for the scene objects' draw distance and the grid cell whose objects it draws. The port lights the stage the same way (`Game.LightStage`, with the stage's own `WorldLights`, and `CutSceneActorView.Light`). Of the 214 chunks the cut-scenes' stages load, 7 hold actors a map places that aren't fullbright, 5 of them within a light, 4 of them torches; the fifth, actor `0x0DAC` in the stage of `0x0392`, changed nothing measurable against `--lights off`, within the scene's own animation's noise.
- **Nothing is drawn** for a light: no glow or flare. The flames are their own scene objects.
- **Checked** in the Gwernia interiors' A01 (`--set 5 --chunk A01 --at 11,10.5`), about 3 from the fire, the chunk's light 19 (radius 5, drifting from (255, 205, 96) to (179, 72, 48)), against `--lights off`: Alaron went from (33.7, 25.8, 19.3) to (45.6, 29.9, 20.9) on average, warm as the fire is, and nothing else changed but the overlay; the townsman beside him, a wanderer, didn't, as he isn't the party's. In Gwernia Castle's M09 by a torch's light (radius 4) he went from (27.0, 20.5, 14.3) to (33.5, 25.1, 16.4). At the spawn, out of every light's reach, he's the same to the byte with and without them.

## A scene's own lights

A Borg5 can list Borg4 lights (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw` (`0x80097a90`) lights a scene by them unless the scene has its engine-lit bit (`+0x1f4` & 0x8000, which `Scene.ResetLights` sets on bodies and the actors maps place) or bit 0x80 (whose only setter, `0x8009df6c`, is never called). Each light's colour is its Borg4's times its transform's tint, clamped, × 255, into both copies; light *k* shines along the third row of transform 1 + *k*'s matrix (× 90). The last is the ambient: with *n* lights it hands the RSP *n* − 1 directional lights (at least 1), so with one alone that's the ambient, beside a black light (`0x800e4748`). A scene with none gets the white ambient (`0x800e4738`) and the black light. Settled from 1.0's machine code (Oct 17, 2026).

- **In the world, never.** No chunk scene and none of the 387 Borg5s that scene objects place has a light (a script over the base plugin and the ROM), so scenery always gets the white ambient. 254 of the 1,402 scenes have lights (1: 9, 2: 227, 3: 4, 4: 12, 5: 2), and 191 of the 203 actors' scenes are among them, but bodies and the actors maps place have the engine-lit bit, so theirs are ignored for the sky's and the point lights.
- **The pause menu's scroll** (`0x0E63`) is drawn without it, so its own three light it: a dim blue (0.19, 0.18, 0.25) and a warm (0.83, 0.73, 0.62) directional light and a black ambient, as the port has it (`N64Material.Lighting.Own`, [ui](ui.md#opening-and-closing)). Its roller in the user's capture of the System tab runs from 0.79 of its texture on the left to 0.19 on the right, which the port matches within 4 a channel on average.

## Draw distance

There are no tiers. Each scene object has a radius, and each of its one or two levels of detail its own distance (`renderProx`), and `RenderVoxelScenes` draws and fades each level every frame. `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.SceneObjectFade` ports it, checked against the debug ROM's machine code, and the runtime's `ChunkStreamer.Fade` applies it each frame. Chunks' own scenes, the terrain, aren't faded: they're drawn out to the far plane, 180, and fogged from about 105.

- **From the player, not the camera.** Every distance is from the player's centre, in 3D (with no player, from the camera's aim). The runtime used to draw levels of detail by Godot's visibility ranges, which measure from the camera. The follow camera is several units behind him, so everything came in several units late. The user found chests far too close (Sep 29, 2026): a chest's closed model is drawn to 10, so it came in when Alaron was a few units from it.
- **An object** isn't drawn from its radius out. Around Gwernia the radius is the last level's distance, except for chests. A chest's object has radius 30 but its model is drawn to 10: a container's object lists the closed, opened and destroyed models, of which only the first is a level of detail. Most of the trees around the spawn are drawn near to 15 and far to 40 or 45, others to 40 and 70, and Oriana's path lights to 5 and 24.
- **A level of detail** is drawn from 2 short of the previous level's distance to its own. It fades out over its last unit, and fades in over its first. So as the player walks away, the next level fades in before this one fades out, and at any distance at most one is part-drawn. The last level fades out over its last unit and the object is gone, rather than popping.
- **The view.** On the ground, an object more than 37.5° off the camera's look fades out by 42.5°, linearly in the angle's cosine, measured from the camera to the object's position. At the follow camera's pitch, a 4:3 screen's corners are about 33° off the look, so it saves drawing what's behind and to the sides without being seen, except for a large object near the camera whose middle is out of view and whose edge isn't. The `DrawWhenNear` flag (0x4) skips this within half the object's radius. A wider window widens the cone by the tangent (`ViewCone.For`), so the fade stays as far outside the screen's edge as it does at 4:3. At 16:9 the original's cone would fade objects visibly at the sides.
- **Between the camera and the player.** An object that isn't `Solid` (flag 0x1), nearer the camera than the player and inside a cone from the player toward the camera, 0.35 of the way back wide, is drawn at 96 of 255 in the middle up to 255 at the cone's edge, whatever the look gave. So bushes and small trees the camera looks through go see-through. Solid ones, including the trees and chests around the spawn, stay.
- **How a faded level is drawn.** Its alpha is the lesser of the view's and its distance's. It's the scene's prim alpha (`SetSceneColors`), so texel alpha times it. Under 255, the scene is drawn after everything else, blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8` in `Scene.SetRenderMode`), testing depth but not writing it. The runtime sets Godot's per-instance `transparency`, which does the same: the transparent pass, depth tested and not written. The N64 material multiplies its own alpha into it. Cut-outs, the foliage and bushes, blend too, since the faded render mode has no alpha test, so texel alpha fades with the rest. Godot draws an alpha-scissor material at full alpha wherever it passes, whatever the instance's transparency, so the leaves stayed on while the trunk faded (the user's check, Sep 29, 2026). A faded model's cut-out surfaces now switch to a blended copy of their material (`N64Material.Faded`) and back when it's whole. The user then found chests, trees, bushes, Oriana's path lights, the one tree near the spawn that isn't solid, and the bonfire outside Gwernia all as on the N64. They blend in on-screen values, as the N64's do (see [on-screen values](#on-screen-values)).
- **Further than the original** is a setting on the options menu's graphics page, Draw distance and Model detail ([ui](ui.md#options)), and the fidelity profile can take it up (`DrawDistance`; for one run, `--draw-distance <scale>` and `--detail nearest`). The scale multiplies each object's radius and each level's distance, and the fades keep their widths. Nearest detail draws each object's first level out to where its last ends, and no other. It passes over a level that draws nothing (`SceneModel.Empty`: every draw is one the game skips, as the export skips them), as the user chose: the first level of Oriana's path lights (`0x1910`) is one, an untextured grey quad whose Borg2 (`0x190F`) has the hidden bit, which only marks where the light goes out, so they're drawn as their billboard from 0 out, and go out within 3 as the original's do. They're the only objects with an empty level. Two limits: the 3×3 chunks are loaded around the player's chunk, so an object can be as little as one chunk (100 in the overworld) away before its chunk is gone, and past about 105 the fog takes everything anyway. So about 1.4× is as far as it goes on the overworld's 70s without loading more chunks and moving the fog.

## Billboards

A Borg5 transform with flag 0x200 is a card the game turns to the camera (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.Billboard` ports the matrix, and the runtime's `Billboards` applies it.

- **What the game draws.** Only the transform's world position and its scale times the scene object's survive. The card rolls by the transform's `rot.z`, turns so its +Z points at the camera's position, not along the way it looks, and is scaled along the world's axes (`guAlignF`). So it tilts back as the camera looks down on it, and it can roll as the camera passes overhead.
- **In the runtime.** The export puts a facing node between the billboard's node and its card. The runtime makes the facing node top-level and, every frame, sets it to the billboard's world position, turned and scaled as the game does. It uses the camera's interpolated position, as the scenery is drawn from. Animations still move and scale the billboard's node and roll the card, and the facing node reads them.
- **Around Gwernia** they're the forest trees' far level of detail, Oriana's path lights between 5 and 24 away, and what look like bushes. Nearly every chunk has some.

## Scrolling textures

Water and fire scroll: the game shifts the texture's bitmap sideways each time a scene loads it (see [textures](../formats/textures.md)). `Aidyn.Core.Scenes.TextureScroll` ports the counter, and the runtime's `TextureScrolls` applies it.

- **The shader** takes how far the image has moved as a share of its width (`scroll`), and subtracts it from S after the material's own clamping or mirroring, then samples with repeat. That wraps the image round inside the texture as the game's shift does. It moves in whole texels, as the original's does, so it steps rather than glides.
- **Each copy of a scene has its own.** As in the original, each copy of a scene gets its own copy of each scrolling texture: the runtime gives it its own materials for them (`TextureScrolls.Attach`).
- **See-through ones.** 206 scenes draw a scrolling texture through a see-through tint, mostly water: Gwernia's sea (0x0EAB) at alpha 0.5, and the light from Gwernia Castle's windows (0x1577 in scene 0x161B, an RGBA16 with no transparent texels) at 0.1. The tint's alpha is the material's prim alpha. Godot's `Resource.Duplicate()` resets a shader parameter set as a `Color` on a `vec4` uniform to the uniform's default, so the copies for each scene drew all of these opaque; the user found the castle's light as solid white slabs (Oct 4, 2026). Prim is now set as a `Vector4`.
- **When it moves.** Once a frame at 20 fps (every 3 ticks, `Cadence.TextureFrameTicks`), each copy of a scene the original would draw loads its scrolling textures, which moves them on. A scene that isn't drawn keeps its place. The original draws the centre chunk's two scenes always, and a neighbour's when the camera's view crosses it (`ChunkView`, from `ChunkView.Drawn`, `0x8000d270` in 1.0) or the player's chunk's map has it drawn whatever the view (`MapRecord.DrawnNeighbours`). The view is a kite of five lines on the ground, 200 long, straight ahead and 42.5° either side. In the chunks it draws, a scene object's levels of detail scroll while they're drawn at all (see [draw distance](#draw-distance)).
- **Neighbours drawn whatever the view.** `RenderZones` (`0x8000d574`) walks the eight neighbours in its table's order (`0x800CD700`: row and column −1 −1, +1 −1, −1 +1, +1 +1, then 0 −1, 0 +1, −1 0, +1 0) with a bit that starts at 1 and doubles, and draws a neighbour, its scene and its objects, when the view crosses it or that bit is set in byte 0x1B of the player's chunk's Borg9 (`Zones.PlayerMap`, `0x800d8288`). `RenderTransZones` (`0x8000dd44`) does the same for the second scenes, from a table of the same eight then the centre (`0x800CD83C`), whose bit has run out of the byte by then, so the centre's second scene is drawn only when the view crosses the centre chunk too. That always happens in chunks 100 across, and only those have second scenes, so it never shows. The import carries the byte as each map's `DrawnNeighbours`, named by compass (rows run east, columns south): 21 chunks of the Overworld set it, from A15 to M22, and one Borg9 no chunk list names; none of Gwernia's. I18 draws its four corners, I19 all but its north-west, south-west and north. Nothing else in 1.0 reads the byte or calls the view test, so wanderers, sounds and the rest don't depend on which chunks are drawn. The only other thing the choice drives: without the Expansion Pak, a neighbour that isn't drawn has its scenes freed when memory runs low (under `0x18000` free).

- **Checked** by two screenshots half a second apart, looking over Gwernia's sea: the water changed and nothing else did. Two screenshots at the same moment were identical. The first port shared one texture between every scene that used it, and the user found Gwernia's sea two to three times too fast against the emulator (Sep 29, 2026). With a copy per scene it moves 2/3 of a texel a frame. The sea is too large to judge by eye, but the fire and the gore on the body outside Gwernia, where the console runs at about 15 fps, still scrolled about twice as fast at the port's 30 fps cadence. So textures scroll at 20 fps instead, the console's average: the user's choice, a middle between spots that chug and interiors that run well, where no texture should look obviously off. The user then found them within a few percent of the original.
- **Where the original differs:** a copy of a scene starts from nothing when it's loaded, so neighbouring chunks' water is out of step in the original, by however far apart they were loaded. The runtime's copies also start together when their chunk loads, but it keeps each scene object's levels of detail for as long as the chunk is loaded. The original frees a level of detail out of range and loads it again later without the Expansion Pak (`NoExpPak_ClearSceneVoxelIndex`), which restarts its water; with it, that isn't checked.

## Particles

The engine's particles ([particles](../formats/particles.md)) are drawn last in the frame, after the deferred scene objects. `ParticlesView` draws what the core's `ParticleHead` drew on its last frame, in the runtime and the editor.

- **The square** (`ParticleHead.Draw`, `0x800a92fc`): four vertices at ±353.55 × 16, scaled by the particle's scale × 0.001 and drawn at 16 to the unit, so a square 0.7071 × its scale on a side, centred on it. It faces along the camera's look and stays upright: one matrix a frame from `guAlign` of the look with no roll (`Particles.DrawPrologue`, `0x800a8f74`), which turns +Z back along the look, the same for every particle, not toward each one. The runtime turns each square by the camera's right and up, which are the same for a camera that doesn't roll. A particle with its own matrix (flag 0x4, the weather's splashes) is turned by that instead.
- **The texture** covers the square once, clamped, with its last row at the top (the vertices' T runs from the height down to 0), so the image shows upside down; the flames are stored that way up. It's filtered bilinearly, in one cycle, so with no mipmap levels.
- **The colour** is the particle's, each channel × 255 and truncated to a byte, after × the screen's brightness when that's under 1, so particles darken with fades, alpha too. It's the vertices' shade: the combiner is `TEXEL0 × SHADE` for colour and alpha (`G_CC_MODULATERGBA`, `0xFC121824 FF33FFFF`), so an intensity texture gives its intensity as both. In on-screen values, as the N64 material is.
- **The blend** is `G_RM_ZB_XLU_SURF` (`0x504A50`): `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, tested against the depth buffer and not written, with no fog, no culling and no lighting (geometry mode `0x200005`). Nothing is sorted: emitters draw in slot order and each one's particles newest first. The runtime draws each slot as one multimesh at a render priority of 100 and up, so after every other translucent draw, unsorted within it and in the original's order between slots.
- **Between frames.** The core steps particles at 30 fps, as the original at that rate; the view moves each one on from where the frame before drew it to where the last did over the next two ticks, so they glide. The view does this itself every frame Godot draws, so its multimeshes are left out of Godot's physics interpolation: that blends each instance from where the last physics tick left the same index, and an index is another particle once one is born or dies, which drew splashes for a frame where others had been and hid them where they were, at any frame rate above the tick's (the movie writer, a tick a frame, never showed it). Measured with `--frames` at 120 fps in the rain at L08, pixels a splash lit for one frame alone went from about 400 a frame to 14.
- **Checked** in the editor's actor view (Oct 17, 2026): Oriana's actor driven with request 5 starts her orb's emitter (scene 11211) after its 40-tick delay, and green and blue stars stream from the orb in her hand as it swings, paling as they fade toward (226, 230, 255) and half alpha.
- **Precipitation.** Rain and snow are two emitters of the same pool that the weather steers ([weather](weather.md#rain-and-snow), `WeatherParticles`), drawn by the same view: streaks and flakes facing the camera, and splashes laid flat on the ground by their own orientation. The original's are sparse and small, a few streaks 0.177 across on screen at once, so the options menu's Rain and snow setting can make each spawn several drops, with a pool to match and the splashes held to 1.5 times the original's. The F3 overlay counts the particles in use, and the drops and splashes the last frame drew, with how many were on screen.

## Normals

- **Unlit meshes have no normals that face up.** They're exported without `NORMAL`, as the N64 doesn't light them, and Godot generates its own. A decal's angle fade (`normal_fade`), which compares the decal with the surface's normal, hid the player's shadow even on level unlit ground, so those generated normals don't face up. Anything that reads unlit geometry's normals will need them exported, or the winding checked.

## What's left

- **The lens flare's test** for what hides the sun uses the collision, not the depth buffer, so foliage doesn't hide it.
- **The projected sky under cameras the follow camera never is.** Cut-scene cameras can look straight up, and a few of the original's shots do. Three things break there, none of them in trek:
  - *Sprites flip overhead.* A patch is kept upright against the horizon by a right-hand side level with the ground and square to its heading. The sun passes straight overhead at noon, and the moon at 09:00 and 21:00. There the game turns the azimuth half round so the object carries on across the sky, which turns the patch's right-hand side round too. The image turns 180° in one frame about its top-left corner, which is what's pinned. So the sun's disc jumps by its own size, and the moon's crescent turns over. Nothing can stay upright against the horizon through the zenith, but taking the right-hand side from the azimuth before it turns would keep the image fixed to the sky. That's how a real sky works: something that rises top-up sets top-down after passing overhead. The clouds reach 53° at most, and the stars are points, so it's only the sun and moon.
  - *The camera is rebuilt from its look.* `SkyScreen.Project` gets only the way the camera looks, and assumes it's level from side to side (no roll) and 45° high. Looking exactly straight up or down draws no sprites at all. A camera that rolls leaves the sky upright while the scenery tilts, and one that pitches over the top draws the sky upside down against the scenery. A camera with another field of view, such as a cinematic shot's, unpins the sky from the scenery. The fix is easy: hand the core the camera's basis and field of view from Godot, not its look alone.
  - *The gradient's dome* is drawn from the camera's own basis and field of view, so it keeps up with any camera, but its top colour fills everything from 17° up: a cut-scene looking up sees a plain sky, where the original's squeezes the whole gradient onto the screen.
- **Rain and snow in cut-scenes** ([weather](weather.md#to-port)). Cut-scenes run the weather, its sky, flash, sounds and fog, but don't draw the precipitation yet; combat's waits for M3. The rest of the weather's look is ported ([weather](weather.md#in-the-port)). Comparing day and night lighting with the console can now tell rain from the time of day.
- **Texture filtering:** probably not worth emulating. Aidyn doesn't use the N64's three-point filtering for artistic effect, and its textures, especially the faces, have far more texels than the screen shows.
- **Animated textures.** No Borg5 uses the scene-level kind, which the game would crash on. The Borg1s that scroll are ported (see [scrolling textures](#scrolling-textures)).
- **Which scenes are drawn**, which also sets how fast textures scroll. Two parts of the original's choice aren't ported:
  - *Its collision grid lists the scene objects to draw* from each square: the player's in the centre chunk, and the squares along a neighbour's edge. The runtime draws every object within its range.
  - *It draws a neighbour chunk only when the camera's view crosses it* or the player's chunk's map has it drawn, objects and all. The runtime draws all nine, and leaves what's out of view to Godot. Only the scroll counts the original's way.
- **Billboards in the editor's model view** face the way they were modelled. Its map set view turns them, as the runtime does.
- **Opened and destroyed containers in the editor.** Once a chest, sack, crate or barrel is opened or destroyed, the original swaps its linked scene object's model for the object's second or third (`replace_container_voxel`). The runtime does ([map-objects](map-objects.md#a-chunks-objects-at-run-time)); the editor's **Flags** switch only leaves the container itself out.
- **Some odd rendering** the user noticed in the cinematics. It isn't pinned down yet.
- **Particles on attached scenes.** 34 emitters sit on a scene combat attaches at run time, a spell's caster and target or an attack's victim (locator bits 7–8); the runtime's anchor gives them no locator yet, which ends them. M3 attaches them.
- **The particles' first pass** (emitter flag 0x1000: after the centre chunk's scene, without the depth buffer, comparing the primitive's depth) isn't drawn apart; nothing the port runs has it.
- **A scene's own lights in the other scenes that have them.** Only the pause menu's scroll uses its Borg4 lights in the port ([a scene's own lights](#a-scenes-own-lights)). The rest that have lights are actors' scenes, which ignore them, or ones the runtime doesn't draw yet, probably spells', combat's and cinematics'; when they're drawn outside a body or a map's actor, they want their own lights too.
- **Combat's point lights.** The party's members are lit by map lights in combat too (+0x70a), which waits for M3. No spell adds a dynamic light in 1.0. Cut-scenes' actors aren't given point lights yet, nor the player in one.
